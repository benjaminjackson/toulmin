# Toulmin, *The Uses of Argument*

Source: Stephen Toulmin, *The Uses of Argument*, updated edition, Cambridge University Press, 2003 (first published 1958). Page numbers are the book's own. Words in quotation marks are Toulmin's. Toulmin calls the facts **data**. The other files call them grounds.

## The stages of an argument (pp. 15 to 21)

The model for a good argument is a lawsuit and not a mathematical proof. A claim may be granted without argument, but its merit rests on the argument that could be produced if someone asks "What have you got to go on?" (pp. 11 to 13).

Every argument, in any field, goes through the same stages.

1. **State the problem.** Ask a clear question. If you cannot yet, say that you are still looking for the question.
2. **Admit the possible answers.** Each suggestion that deserves a hearing gets one.
3. **Weigh them against what is known.** This can come out four ways, and each has its own word. One answer is left standing: it **must** be so. Some answers are struck out: they **cannot** be so. The remaining answers are ranked: one is **probable**. Or an answer holds unless some unusual condition applies: it is a **presumption**.

### Admitting and dismissing a rival answer

- To call a rival view "possible" is to promise it a hearing: "to speak of a particular suggestion as a possibility is to concede that it has a right to be considered" (p. 17).
- "To call something 'possible' and then to ignore it indefinitely without good reason is inconsistent" (p. 18). Calling it "not possible" and then spending pages on it is inconsistent too. The one fair exception is a remote possibility set aside until the stronger ones are dealt with.
- "Possible" means more than "not self-contradictory." A rival that no informed reader would take seriously does not need a hearing (p. 34).
- "Cannot" is an instruction to rule something out, and it needs conclusive reasons (p. 27). The pattern for dismissing a rival: "P being what it is, you must rule out anything involving Q: to do otherwise would be R, and would invite S" (p. 28). P is your ground, Q is what you rule out, R is the fault in ignoring the ground, and S is what would follow. A dismissal that cannot fill in P, R and S is only an assertion.

## The layout of an argument (Essay III)

Each part answers a different challenge to the claim (pp. 90 to 96).

- **Data** answer "What have you got to go on?"
- **The warrant** answers "How do you get there?" It is a rule or principle that licenses the step: one of the "general, hypothetical statements, which can act as bridges" (p. 91).
- **Backing** answers "But why do you think that?", aimed at the warrant.

The whole layout in a line (p. 97): **D, so Q, C. Since W. Unless R. On account of B.**

**"How do you get there?" is not a request for more facts.** Adding data does not answer it, because the same question can be asked about the new data. Only a general statement of the form "data such as D entitle one to draw conclusions such as C" answers it (p. 91). When a reader is not persuaded and the writer responds by piling on evidence, the writer has usually misread which challenge the reader is making.

**If the data are disputed, that has to be settled first,** by a separate argument (p. 90).

### The standard example

Harry, born in Bermuda (pp. 92 to 97).

- Data: Harry was born in Bermuda.
- Warrant: A man born in Bermuda will generally be a British subject.
- Backing: The statutes on the nationality of persons born in the British colonies.
- Qualifier and claim: So, presumably, Harry is a British subject.
- Rebuttal: Unless both his parents were aliens, or he has become a naturalised American.

### Telling data, warrant and backing apart

An argument checked with the wrong label on a part gets the wrong diagnosis.

- **Data are stated. Warrants are usually not.** "Data are appealed to explicitly, warrants implicitly" (p. 92).
- **Data are particular. Warrants are general.** A warrant certifies every argument of its type. The parallel is the split in a courtroom between questions of fact and questions of law (p. 92).
- **A warrant does not report a fact. It gives permission.** It is "a general moral of a practical character, about the ways in which we can safely argue in view of these facts" (p. 98).
- **Backing is plain fact.** That an Act of a given date contains a given provision is a straightforward fact (p. 98).
- **Test by the job the sentence does, and not by its grammar.** "Whenever A, one *has found* that B" is data or backing. "Whenever A, one *may take it* that B" is a warrant (p. 92).
- **Watch "All A's are B's."** It can mean "every single A has been found to be a B," which is backing, or "an A can be taken to be a B," which is a warrant. Say which (pp. 101 to 104).

Backing says why the warrant is acceptable in general. The qualifier says how much force the warrant lends in this case. The rebuttal names an exception that defeats it (p. 99).

**The same form of warrant takes different backing in each field** (p. 96). "A whale will be a mammal" is backed by a system of classification, "A Bermudan will be a Briton" by statute, and "A Saudi Arabian will be a Muslim" by statistics.

### When each part has to appear

- **Data must always be given.** "A bare conclusion, without any data produced in its support, is no argument" (p. 98).
- **Backing can stay unstated until someone challenges the warrant.** "If we demanded the credentials of all warrants at sight and never let one pass unchallenged, argument could scarcely begin" (p. 98).

### An extra fact can do one of two jobs

A further fact can be more data, or it can show whether the warrant applies (p. 95). That Harry's parents were not aliens adds nothing to the data. It removes a rebuttal. When adding a fact, know which job it is for.

Whether a step is done correctly and whether it is the right step are separate questions. "Is this calculation mathematically impeccable?" is a different question from "Is this the relevant calculation?" (p. 95).

### Is the reader's warrant already in place, or does it have to be built?

- A **warrant-using** argument applies a warrant the audience already accepts to new data.
- A **warrant-establishing** argument shows that a new warrant works, by applying it to cases where the data and the conclusion have both been checked independently. In it "the warrant, not the conclusion, is novel, and so on trial" (p. 112).

This is the writer's first decision. If the reader accepts the bridge, supply the facts and cross it. If the reader does not, more facts will not help. The piece has to establish the warrant first, with cases the reader can check for themselves.

### Real arguments are not proofs, and that is not a weakness

In nearly all practical argument the conclusion is a different kind of statement from its support: a claim about the future drawn from the past, a claim about someone's legal position, a moral or political position (pp. 115 to 116). It can never be a restatement of the evidence. It can still be conclusive.

- Do not apologise for an argument because it falls short of proof. That a conclusion does not follow by strict logic "is nothing to regret, or to apologise for" (p. 231).
- Judge an argument by the standards of its field. "Validity is an intra-field, not an inter-field notion" (p. 235).
- If a critic's standard would make every argument in a field invalid, the standard is what is wrong (p. 236).
- A practical argument needs a presumption strong enough that doubting it would be unreasonable. "Some things it is more unreasonable to doubt than to believe" (p. 230).

## Force and criteria (pp. 28 to 37)

Any word of assessment, such as "cannot," "good," "possible" or "probably," has two sides.

- Its **force** is what using it does. "Cannot" tells the hearer to rule something out. "Good" commends. Force is the same in every field.
- Its **criteria** are the grounds a speaker must be able to give for using it. These differ from field to field. You cannot lift a ton single-handed, cannot smoke in this compartment, and cannot have a male sister, on three different kinds of ground.

Two mistakes this guards against. One is to treat a single field's standard as the only real one, and to judge a case for a painting by the standard of a laboratory. The other is to conclude that some fields, such as ethics or taste, are beyond reasoned assessment. None is (p. 37). Arguments from different fields can each be judged strong or weak by their own standards. They cannot all be ranked on one scale (p. 36).

## How strongly to state a claim (Essay II)

### "Probably" is a guarded commitment

Saying "probably" does not report how confident the writer feels. It makes the commitment with a guard on it (p. 46). The guard is like insurance, and the insurance has terms (pp. 46 to 48).

- **Name what you are guarding against.** The cover extends to that condition and no further.
- **If that condition does not arise and the claim still fails, you are as answerable as if you had said it flat.** "I only said probably" is then bad faith.
- **You have to be right most of the time.** A separate excuse for each failure does not rescue a writer who is often wrong after saying "probably."
- **You cannot take back with the second half of the sentence what you gave with the first.** "It will probably work, but it won't" is a contradiction.
- **A "probably" with nothing behind it is hedging.**

"In all probability" is stronger than "probably" and has to hold nearly every time (p. 61).

### The evidence decides which word you may use

"It is the quality of the evidence or argument at the speaker's disposal which determines what sort of qualifier he is entitled to include in his statements" (p. 83). The choice among "certainly," "probably" and "possibly" is not a matter of tone. A person who has seen one painting has no right to "probably his finest."

Qualifiers tell the reader how far to rely on the claim (pp. 83 to 84).

### "I know" gives your word

"I know" is not the top of a scale of confidence. Saying it puts the speaker's authority behind the claim, as "I promise" does for an intention (pp. 45 to 46). So "I know, but I may be wrong" does not work. A plain flat statement carries the same weight. A writer who is not prepared to stand behind a flat statement should qualify it.

### A claim can be proper and still turn out wrong

There are two ways to fault a claim (pp. 53 to 57).

- It was **mistaken**. The evidence is what happened afterwards.
- It was **improper**: the person had no business making it. The evidence is their standing or their grounds at the time.

These come apart. A claim can be properly made and prove wrong, or be improperly made and prove right by luck. A claim is proper when the person making it has enough experience, has made every observation and test that could reasonably be asked for, and has judged soundly. That makes it "a fit basis for action," even though later events may reopen it (pp. 56 to 57).

When attacking a claim, decide which fault you allege. When defending a past recommendation that turned out badly, the fair question is what was known when it was made.

### Qualifier and rebuttal both describe the warrant

Some warrants let us accept a claim "unequivocally." Others allow it only "tentatively, or else subject to conditions, exceptions, or qualifications" (p. 93).

Keep the warrant and the statement of when it applies as two separate sentences: "A man born in Bermuda will be British" and "This presumption holds good provided his parents were not both aliens" (p. 94). This matters most where a rule has known exceptions or rests on a correlation.

Rules of conduct are of this kind. Moral rules "can authorise only presumptive conclusions" (p. 109). Word an argument from a principle as a presumption that an opposing consideration could outweigh.

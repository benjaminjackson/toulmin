# Worked examples from Karbach's paper

Source: Joan Karbach, "Using Toulmin's Model of Argumentation," *Journal of Teaching Writing*, pages 81 to 91. The six parts and the questions are in `SKILL.md`. This file holds the paper's worked answers, which show what a good answer to each question looks like.

## Building an argument: the six questions answered

A writer wants restaurant owners to replace tipping with a 15% service charge.

1. **What position do I want my audience to take?** *Claim: A 15% service charge should be added to patrons' checks in place of tipping.*
2. **Where must my audience begin so that they will take the step I want?** Business owners respond to anything that helps them keep staff. *Grounds: Waitresses who make a dependable wage will be less likely to leave. Patrons no longer have to work out how much to tip, and service staff share the income fairly.*
3. **What is the idea that links grounds and claim?** An appeal to the owners' profits will help, so the writer decides to state it and not leave it implied. *Warrant: High turnover of waitresses is costly.*
4. **Is the move from grounds to claim safe and reliable?** Every employer already accepts this. *Backing: The paperwork and training involved in hiring new employees take time and money.*
5. **What possibilities might upset the argument?** *Rebuttal: At first, the waitresses' reaction to the new policy may lead to poor service.*
6. **Is a qualification necessary?** Yes, in case the waitresses resist. *Qualifier: So, presumably...*

Two things to notice.

**The grounds are chosen for the audience.** Restaurant owners already care about staff turnover, so the argument starts there. The same claim put to a different audience would start from different grounds.

**The rebuttal produced a second argument.** "The waitresses may react badly" names a second audience that has to be persuaded. That gives a new claim addressed to them: "A 15% service charge added to patrons' checks will ensure you a steady income." The rebuttal is where a new argument most often comes from.

## Checking an argument: the four questions answered

The same argument, checked in the order claim, rebuttal, backing, grounds.

1. **Claim. Is it clearly understood, and from what standpoint is it made?** "Added automatically" might read better as "built-in." The standpoint is financial.
2. **Rebuttal. Does the wording of the claim allow for the exceptions?** It does not. So the claim is reworded: "A 15% service charge, added automatically to patrons' checks, *may* be beneficial to restaurateurs." The word "may" covers the case where the waitresses resist.
3. **Backing. Is the warrant solidly backed?** Yes. Hiring and training cost time and money.
4. **Grounds. Are they sufficient and relevant?** Yes, given the warrant and backing.

## When to state the warrant

A student argues that a commuter campus needs a frequent, dependable bus service.

- Grounds: 10,000 students attend. It is a commuter campus. Parking is limited.
- Warrant: Additional parking lots and similar fixes are not feasible.
- Claim: A frequent, dependable transit system is necessary to serve the campus.

If the readers are the campus administrators, they already know more parking cannot be built, and the warrant can stay implied. If the reader is the director of the transit system, who does not know the campus, the warrant has to be stated. Without it the facts do not lead to the claim.

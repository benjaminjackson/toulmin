**First period is where our students are struggling. Let's move it.**

Every school day, the first hour is the busiest in my office.

Last fall I logged 412 student visits during first period: headaches, stomachaches, kids who couldn't keep their heads up at their desks. Second period brought 198. Third brought 171. First period alone outpaced the next two combined.

I'm the nurse at [School], and I no longer think of that as a coincidence. When the school board votes April 14 on moving our start time from 7:25 to 8:30, I hope it votes yes.

The reason shows up in our students' own answers. In a survey run by our health class, 640 of the 890 students who responded (72 percent) said they sleep less than seven hours on a school night. Teenagers' body clocks shift later during adolescence; most can't fall asleep earlier just by trying. A 7:25 bell doesn't move their bedtime. It only cuts their sleep short, and they arrive running on empty. I see what that looks like up close. So do their first-period teachers.

That is why the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that high schools start no earlier than 8:30. This isn't a fringe position. It's the recommendation of the nation's pediatricians.

We also don't have to guess how this plays out close to home. A district two counties over moved to 8:30 three years ago. Their first-period tardies fell 31 percent, and their superintendent told our board that attendance rose about a point.

Here's why that matters for graduation. Our four-year graduation rate is 84 percent. That means roughly one student in six doesn't finish on time. Students rarely fall off track in a single day. They fall behind one missed class at a time: late arrivals, failed first-period courses, credits that never get earned. A schedule that puts more students in their seats, awake, for the first class of the day goes straight at that problem.

I won't promise the board a specific number. Nobody honestly can. But I'm asking the board to make graduation the goal and to measure it. After the first year, report first-period tardies, attendance and course failures. Then track the graduation rate of the first classes who spend all of high school on the new schedule. If those numbers don't move, the community deserves to know. I expect they will.

The concerns on the other side are real, and the board should face them squarely.

Athletics will be harder. Our athletic director is right that practices would end after dark from November on and that away games would pull some athletes out of last period. Those are real scheduling problems. The board can ask the district two counties over how it has handled practices and away games for three years. They're a reason to plan carefully, not a reason to keep every student in a classroom at 7:25.

Families who drop their kids off on the way to 8 a.m. shifts need a real answer, too. One option is opening the library or cafeteria with supervision from 7:30, so those students have somewhere safe to be. I'd ask the board to price that alongside the buses.

The district says the new bus contract would cost an extra $180,000 a year. That's real money, and I don't dismiss it. But the board should weigh it against what the current schedule already costs us: 412 trips to the nurse's office in one fall's first periods, and a graduation rate that leaves one in six students without a diploma after four years.

I spend my days with the students who aren't doing OK. Many of them aren't sick. They're exhausted. We have the evidence from our own school, the recommendation of the nation's pediatricians, and a nearby district that has already made the change.

On April 14, I hope the board gives our students that hour back.

*[Name] is the school nurse at [School].*

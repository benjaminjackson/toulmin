**Let our teenagers sleep: Start the high school day at 8:30**

I'm the nurse at [School Name]. If you ask me which class period sends the most students to my office, the answer is first period, and it isn't close.

Last fall I logged 412 visits during first period. Students came in with headaches and stomachaches, and some simply couldn't keep their eyes open. Second period brought 198 visits and third period brought 171. First period alone sent me more students than the next two combined.

Our students can tell you why. When our health class surveyed the student body, 640 of the 890 students who answered said they sleep less than seven hours on a school night. That's nearly three out of four. It isn't laziness. During adolescence the body clock shifts later, and most teenagers can't fall asleep early however firmly we tell them to. That's why the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that high schools start no earlier than 8:30 a.m. Ours starts at 7:25.

On April 14 the school board will vote on moving our start time to 8:30. I urge them to vote yes.

We don't have to guess what happens next. [Neighboring District], two counties over, made this change three years ago. Their first-period tardies dropped 31 percent. Their superintendent told our board that attendance went up about a point.

Here's why that matters. Our four-year graduation rate is 84 percent, so roughly one in six of our students doesn't finish on time. Graduation isn't lost on one bad morning. It's lost over hundreds of them: a first-period class missed, a quiz slept through, a credit that slips away one tardy at a time. A later start won't fix that by itself, and I won't pretend it will. But it goes straight at the hours where I see students struggling most. A student who is in class and awake has a better chance of walking across that stage.

The concerns about this change are real, and the board should face them openly.

Athletics: our athletic director points out that from November on, practices would end after dark, and away games would pull athletes out of last-period classes. Those are scheduling problems, and [Neighboring District] has been solving them for three years. Before April 14, our district should ask them how.

Working parents: many families drop their kids off on the way to 8 a.m. shifts. The district could keep the building open and supervised from 7:30, which would give those students a safe, quiet place to start the day. For some it might be the only quiet hour they get.

Cost: the district estimates the new bus contract would cost an extra $180,000 a year. That's real money and the board should weigh it seriously. Weigh it against the alternative, too: three out of four students starting every school day short on sleep, and a nurse's office that fills up before second period.

I'd ask the board for one more thing. Don't just vote for 8:30. Commit to measuring it. Track first-period tardies, attendance, visits to my office and the graduation rate, and report them to the public every year. If the numbers don't move, revisit the decision. If they do, as they did in [Neighboring District], we'll know the money was well spent.

I see these students before most adults in their day do. I see the ones who came in without breakfast, the ones with a headache that is really exhaustion, and the ones who put their heads down on my cot and are asleep in a minute. They aren't failing school. The schedule is failing them.

Please tell the board before April 14 that you support an 8:30 start. Our students will be better rested, healthier and more likely to be in class, and that is how a student graduates.

*[Your Name] is the school nurse at [School Name].*

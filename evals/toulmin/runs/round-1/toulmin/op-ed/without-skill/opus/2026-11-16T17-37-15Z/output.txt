**Let our teenagers sleep: Start the high school at 8:30**

Every school morning, before most of the building has fully woken up, my office starts filling.

I'm the nurse at [School Name], and last fall I kept a log. During first period I recorded 412 visits: headaches, stomachaches, students who had fallen asleep at their desks and couldn't shake it. In second period I saw 198. In third, 171. First period alone outnumbered the next two combined.

Those students aren't faking, and they aren't lazy. They're tired. When our health class surveyed students, 640 of the 890 who answered (72 percent) said they sleep less than seven hours on a school night. Teenagers need eight to ten.

This isn't about willpower. During adolescence, the body's internal clock shifts later. Most teens can't fall asleep until around 11 p.m., no matter how early they go to bed. A 7:25 start has many of them waking around 6, which means they arrive at school in the middle of their biological night. That's why the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that high schools start no earlier than 8:30 a.m.

On April 14, our school board will vote on doing exactly that. I urge them to vote yes.

We don't have to guess what happens next. [Neighboring District], two counties over, moved its high school start to 8:30 three years ago. First-period tardies dropped 31 percent. Their superintendent told our board that attendance rose by "about a point."

That matters for more than my office log. Our four-year graduation rate is 84 percent, which means roughly one in six of our students doesn't finish on time. Missing class and falling behind are among the clearest warning signs that a student is at risk of not graduating. I can't promise that a later bell will move our graduation rate by a particular number, and no one honestly can. But a change that gets more students into class on time and awake works directly on the things that keep kids on track to finish. I'd ask the board to measure it: if we make this change, let's track attendance, tardies and graduation, and report the results to the public.

The objections are real, and I don't dismiss them.

Our athletic director points out that from November on, practices would end after dark, and that away games would pull athletes out of last-period classes. Many parents drop their kids off on the way to 8 a.m. shifts. And the district estimates the bus contract would cost an extra $180,000 a year.

Each of these deserves a plan, not a veto. Districts that have made this switch faced the same scheduling problems, and our board should ask our neighbors how they handled athletics. For working parents, the building could open early with a supervised space and breakfast, so no student is left waiting on the sidewalk. And $180,000 a year comes to about $1,000 per school day. That's a real cost, and it should be weighed against the cost of students who are absent, asleep, or sitting in my office instead of in class.

What we can't do is keep treating exhaustion as normal. Every morning I hand out ice packs and crackers to kids whose real problem is that we've asked them to learn at an hour their bodies aren't built for. We know what the pediatricians recommend. We've seen what happened next door.

On April 14, give our students the extra hour. They'll use it.

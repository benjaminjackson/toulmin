<document>
**What I See in the Nurse's Office Before 8:30 a.m.**

I've been the school nurse at [School] for [X] years. On April 14, the school board will vote on moving our high school start time from 7:25 to 8:30. I hope the board votes yes, and I want to explain why, based on what I see from my office.

Last fall I kept a log. During first period, students came to my office 412 times with headaches or stomachaches, or because a teacher found them asleep at their desks. During second period, that number was 198. During third, 171. First period alone sent me more students than the next two periods combined.

A log can't tell me exactly why. But when our health class surveyed students, 640 of the 890 who responded, or 72 percent, said they sleep less than seven hours on school nights. Teenagers need eight to ten. I don't think the pattern in my office is a coincidence.

This isn't about lazy teenagers. During adolescence, the body's internal clock shifts later, and most teens physically struggle to fall asleep early. A 7:25 bell means many of our students wake up around 6 a.m., and bus riders even earlier. That's why the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that high schools start no earlier than 8:30.

We don't have to guess how this works in practice. [District name], two counties over, moved to 8:30 three years ago. Their first-period tardies dropped 31 percent, and their superintendent told our board that attendance rose about a point.

That matters for more than groggy mornings. Our four-year graduation rate is 84 percent, which means about one in six of our students doesn't finish on time. I won't pretend that a later bell alone will fix that. But students who miss class or sleep through it fall behind, and falling behind is how students end up not graduating. A schedule that gets more students through the door, awake and ready to learn, is a sensible step toward helping more of them finish.

The concerns about this change are real, and I don't want to dismiss them.

Our athletic director points out that from November on, practices would end after dark, and away games would pull athletes out of last-period classes. Many parents drop their kids off on their way to 8 a.m. shifts. And the district estimates the bus contract would cost an extra $180,000 a year.

These are problems to solve, not reasons to stop. Before the vote, the board can ask [District name] how it handled practice schedules, away games and early drop-off, since that district faced the same questions three years ago. Families who need to drop off early deserve a supervised place for their kids to wait, and a plan for that should be part of any proposal. Athletes deserve a game schedule that protects their class time as much as possible.

As for the $180,000, that's real money and the board should weigh it carefully. But we should also count what we're already paying: class time lost to students sitting in my office, teachers re-teaching material that sleeping students missed, and the long-term cost to a young person who doesn't finish high school.

I'm a nurse, not a sleep scientist or a budget officer. What I can tell you is what I see every morning: kids holding their heads, kids who can't keep their eyes open, kids who tell me they got five hours of sleep. Then, after first period, the line at my office gets shorter.

We ask our students to show up ready to learn. We should give them a schedule that makes that possible. I urge the board to vote yes on April 14, and I urge parents and community members to attend that meeting and say so.

*[Name] is the school nurse at [School].*

**Our students are telling us something at 7:30 a.m. We should listen.**

I'm the nurse at [School Name] High School. Every school day, before most of our students have finished their first class, my office starts to fill up.

Last fall I logged 412 visits during first period alone: headaches, stomachaches, and students who had fallen asleep at their desks and couldn't get their heads up. That's more than second period (198) and third period (171) put together. By midmorning the line at my door gets shorter. The kids haven't gotten healthier in two hours. They've woken up.

Ask them why, and the answer is almost always the same: they're exhausted. In a recent survey run by our health class, 640 of the 890 students who responded said they sleep less than seven hours on school nights. That's nearly three out of four. Teenagers need eight to ten hours. And this isn't simply about phones or late nights. During adolescence, the body's internal clock shifts later. A 16-year-old told to fall asleep at 9:30 is often lying awake, just like an adult would be if told to go to bed at 7:30.

That's why the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that high schools start no earlier than 8:30 a.m. Ours starts at 7:25. On April 14, the school board will vote on whether to close that gap.

We don't have to guess what happens when a school makes the change. A district two counties over moved its high school start to 8:30 three years ago. First-period tardies dropped 31 percent, and their superintendent told our board that attendance went up about a point.

That matters for a number this community should care about. Our four-year graduation rate is 84 percent. That means roughly one in six of our students doesn't finish on time. I won't pretend a later bell will fix that by itself. Students leave school for many reasons, and no single policy solves them all. But I see what wears them down: missed mornings, failed first-period classes, and kids too tired to keep up who fall behind and stop believing they can catch up. Students who miss school fall behind, and students who fall behind are the ones we lose. A schedule that gets more of them into their seats, awake and able to learn, is one of the most direct tools this board has for keeping kids on track to graduate.

The concerns about the change are real, and they deserve straight answers.

The district estimates the new bus contract would cost an extra $180,000 a year. That's real money. It's also a decision about what we value. We regularly spend money on things meant to help students succeed, and few of them touch every student, every day, the way the start time does.

Our athletic director has pointed out that from November on, practices would end after dark, and some away games would pull athletes out of their last class. Many parents drop their kids off on the way to 8 a.m. shifts. These are scheduling problems, and scheduling problems can be solved: supervised early-arrival space and breakfast for students who are dropped off early, adjusted practice schedules, and working with the conference on game times. The district two counties over faced the same issues. Before April 14, our board should ask them exactly how they handled each one.

What we shouldn't do is keep asking teenagers to fight their own biology five days a week, then wonder why they're in my office with their heads in their hands at 7:45.

I'd urge parents, students, and neighbors to attend the April 14 meeting or contact board members before then. Ask them to vote for an 8:30 start. Our kids are already telling us they need it. Every morning, they show up at my door to say so.

*[Your Name] is the school nurse at [School Name] High School.*

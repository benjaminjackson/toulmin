**Let our teenagers sleep: Vote yes on 8:30**

Every school day, before much of our town has finished its first cup of coffee, a line forms outside my office.

I'm the nurse at [School]. Last fall I logged 412 visits during first period alone: headaches, stomachaches, students who nodded off at their desks and couldn't shake it. That's more than second period (198) and third period (171) combined. Something about 7:25 a.m. is making our kids sick, and I don't think the cause is a mystery.

When our health class surveyed students, 640 of the 890 who responded, about 72 percent, said they sleep less than seven hours on school nights. Teenagers need eight to ten. This isn't simply a matter of willpower. In adolescence, the body's internal clock shifts later, and many teens struggle to fall asleep before about 11 p.m. no matter when we send them to bed. A 7:25 bell means an alarm around 6. The math doesn't work.

That's why the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that high schools start no earlier than 8:30. On April 14, our school board can follow that advice. It should.

We don't have to guess how it goes. A district two counties over moved to 8:30 three years ago. Their first-period tardies fell 31 percent, and their superintendent told our board that attendance rose "about a point."

Those numbers point to something bigger. Our four-year graduation rate is 84 percent, which means roughly one in six of our students doesn't finish on time. Students rarely drop out all at once. They drift: a missed first period, then a missed morning, then a failed class they kept sleeping through. Attendance is one of the earliest warning signs educators watch for. I can't promise a later bell will raise our graduation rate by a set amount, and no one honestly can. But it goes straight at one of the forces that pushes kids off track, and I would rather we act on that than accept the line outside my door as normal.

The concerns about this change are real, and I won't pretend otherwise.

Our athletic director is right that from November on, practices would end after dark and away games would pull athletes out of last-period classes. That deserves solutions: lighting, adjusted practice schedules, and coordination with teachers so athletes don't fall behind. But it affects some students for part of the year. Sleep loss affects nearly all of them, every day.

Many parents drop their kids off on the way to 8 a.m. shifts. The district should keep the building open and supervised early, with the library or cafeteria available as a calm place to start the day, so no family has to choose between a job and a safe drop-off.

And the bus contract would cost an extra $180,000 a year. That's real money, and the board should say plainly where it would come from. But we're already paying for the current schedule in instructional time lost to tardies, in students sitting in my office instead of class, and in the long-term cost to a young person who doesn't graduate.

Every week I send students back to class with an ice pack or a few crackers, knowing that what they really need is sleep. I can't give them that. The board can.

On April 14, I hope board members vote to start our high school at 8:30, and then do the follow-through work that makes it succeed for athletes, working families and taxpayers alike. Our students' bodies have been telling us what they need. It's time we listened.

*[Name] is the school nurse at [School].*

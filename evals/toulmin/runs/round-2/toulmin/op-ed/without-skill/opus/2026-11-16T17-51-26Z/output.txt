**Let our teenagers sleep: Start high school at 8:30**

*By [Name], school nurse, [School] High School*

Every school morning, my office fills up before most adults have finished their coffee.

Last fall I logged 412 visits during first period alone: headaches, stomachaches, students who put their heads down and couldn't stay awake. That's more than second period (198) and third period (171) combined. By midmorning, the line outside my door shrinks. The kids haven't changed. The hour has.

That pattern isn't a coincidence. During adolescence, the body's internal clock shifts later. Most teenagers can't fall asleep much before 11 p.m., no matter when they go to bed. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that high schools start no earlier than 8:30 a.m. Ours starts at 7:25.

Our students are telling us what that costs. In a survey run by our health class, 640 of the 890 students who responded, or 72 percent, said they sleep less than seven hours on school nights. Teenagers need eight to ten.

On April 14, the school board will vote on moving our start time to 8:30. I hope they vote yes.

We don't have to guess what happens next. A district two counties over made this change three years ago. Their first-period tardies fell 31 percent, and their superintendent told our board that attendance rose about a point.

Attendance matters because it's where graduation begins. Our four-year graduation rate is 84 percent, which means roughly one in six of our students doesn't finish on time. I can't promise the board that a later bell will raise that number by a specific amount, and no one honestly can. But students who are in their seats and awake are students who pass their classes, and students who pass their classes graduate. Missed mornings and failed first-period courses are exactly the kind of quiet slide that ends with a senior short on credits. I see the start of that slide in my office every day.

The concerns about this change are real, and I don't want to wave them away.

Our athletic director is right that practices would end after dark from November on, and that away games would pull athletes out of last-period classes. That deserves a real plan: lighted fields, rotating which class gets missed, and working with conference schools on game times. The district two counties over still fields teams. We should ask how they did it.

Many parents drop their kids off on the way to 8 a.m. shifts. The district can open the building early with supervised space, such as the library, cafeteria or gym, so no family has to choose between a job and a safe place for their child.

And the bus contract would cost an extra $180,000 a year. That's real money, and the board should be clear-eyed about it. But it buys every student in the building an hour of sleep, five days a week, for the whole year.

These are logistics problems, and they have logistics answers. The sleep problem doesn't, at least not as long as we ask teenagers' bodies to do something they aren't built to do at 7:25 in the morning.

So I'd ask every board member to spend one first period in my office before April 14. Watch who comes through the door, and listen to why. Then vote to let them sleep.

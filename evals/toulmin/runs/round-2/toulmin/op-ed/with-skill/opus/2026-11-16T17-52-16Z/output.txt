**I see what a 7:25 bell does to our students. The board should move it.**

*By [Name], school nurse at [School Name]*

Most school mornings, students are lined up outside my office before first period is half over. Last fall I logged 412 visits during first period alone: headaches, stomachaches, students who fell asleep at their desks and couldn't be woken up enough to work. That's more than second and third period combined, which had 198 and 171. By mid-morning the line is mostly gone. It's the same students, the same building and the same nurse. What changes is how long they've been awake.

On April 14, the school board votes on moving our high school's start time from 7:25 to 8:30. I'm asking the board to vote yes.

Our students aren't getting enough sleep. In a survey our health class ran this year, 640 of the 890 students who answered, nearly three in four, said they sleep less than seven hours on school nights. That isn't a discipline problem. During adolescence the body's clock shifts later, so a teenager sent to bed at 9:30 often lies awake until 11 no matter what the house rules say. An early bell doesn't make bedtime earlier. It only makes the night shorter. That's why the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that high schools start no earlier than 8:30.

We don't have to guess what happens when a district follows that advice. A district two counties over moved to 8:30 three years ago. Their first-period tardies dropped 31 percent, and their superintendent told our board that attendance went up about a point. That's more students in their seats, on time, at the start of the day.

That matters because of another number: our four-year graduation rate is 84 percent. About one student in six doesn't finish on time. I won't pretend a bell schedule alone will change that. But I've watched students drift off track, and it rarely happens in a single day. It starts with mornings missed, a first-period class failed because the student was asleep or in my office, and a credit that has to be made up later. A later start goes straight at those mornings. Every first period a student spends awake and in class is one less hole to dig out of on the way to a diploma.

The objections are real, and the board should weigh them honestly.

The district estimates the new bus contract will cost $180,000 a year. That's real money. But the current schedule has costs too. Under a 7:25 start, we can expect another fall with hundreds of first-period visits to my office, another year where most of our students are short on sleep, and another class where one in six doesn't graduate on time. The board should weigh $180,000 against that, not against zero.

Our athletic director has pointed out that practices would end after dark from November on and that away games would pull athletes out of last period. Those are fair concerns. A district a short drive away has run on an 8:30 start for three years. Before April 14, the board should ask them how they schedule practices and games, rather than assume the problem can't be solved.

Many parents drop their kids off on the way to 8 a.m. shifts, and a later bell can't leave those families stranded. The district should open the building at 7:30 with adult supervision so students have a safe place to eat breakfast, read or finish homework. Those families' mornings stay the same, and everyone else's students gain an hour.

I'm not a researcher or a board member. I'm the person students come to when their bodies tell them something is wrong, and in first period, their bodies are telling us. Please tell the board before April 14, and vote for 8:30.

*[Name] is the school nurse at [School Name]. The views expressed are their own.*

**Our first-period students are telling us something. The board should listen.**

Every school morning, my office fills up before most adults have finished their coffee.

I'm the nurse at [School]. Last fall I logged 412 student visits during first period: headaches, stomachaches, kids who had fallen asleep at their desks. That's more than second period (198) and third period (171) put together. Same school, same day. By mid-morning the line at my door is less than half as long. What changes is how long students have been awake.

They aren't getting enough sleep. When our health classes surveyed students, 640 of the 890 who answered, or 72 percent, said they sleep less than seven hours on school nights. Teenagers' sleep cycles shift later during adolescence, which is why the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that high schools start no earlier than 8:30. Ours starts at 7:25.

On April 14, the school board will vote on moving our start to 8:30. I'm asking board members to vote yes.

We don't have to guess what happens next. A district two counties over made the switch three years ago. First-period tardies fell 31 percent. Their superintendent told our board that attendance went up "about a point."

That matters because attendance is where graduation starts. Our four-year graduation rate is 84 percent, which means about one student in six doesn't finish on time. A student who misses first period, or sits through it with their head on the desk, falls behind. Students who fall behind are the ones we lose.

I won't claim an 8:30 start will raise our graduation rate by some exact number. No one can promise that yet, and our neighbors have not yet graduated a class that spent all four years under the later bell. What I can say is that a later start goes after the problem at its first link: the tired student who isn't in class, or isn't awake for it. And we can check whether it's working long before graduation day.

So I'm asking the board to do two things: vote for 8:30, and commit to reporting first-period attendance, tardies and nurse visits to the public after the first year. If those numbers don't improve, the board should revisit the decision. If they do, we'll know we're on the path to graduating more of our kids.

The concerns about the change are real, and they deserve straight answers.

The bus contract would cost an extra $180,000 a year. That is real money, and the board should weigh it openly. But keeping 7:25 has a cost too. We pay it in lost class time, in 412 first-period trips to my office, and in students who never quite catch up.

Our athletic director is right that practices would end after dark from November on, and that away games would pull some athletes out of last period. Our neighbors play under the same winter sunset we do. Before April 14, the board should ask them how they've scheduled around it and adopt what works.

Many parents drop their kids off on the way to 8 a.m. shifts. Those families shouldn't have to choose between their jobs and their children's sleep. Opening the library or cafeteria at 7:15 with an adult on duty would give those students a safe, quiet place to start the day. The board should cost that out alongside the buses.

None of these problems is a reason to keep asking teenagers to be alert at 7:25. They're logistics, and logistics can be worked out. A teenager can't simply try harder to need less sleep.

I see the results of our current schedule every morning, one student at a time. On April 14, the board can change it.

*[Name], RN, is the school nurse at [School].*

<document>
**Our students are exhausted. An 8:30 start can help.**

Every school morning, my office fills up before the rest of the building is fully awake. As the nurse at [High School], I see students with headaches and upset stomachs, and some who simply can't keep their heads up. Last fall I logged 412 visits during first period. Second and third period combined came to 369.

On April 14, the school board will vote on moving our high school start time from 7:25 to 8:30. I urge a yes vote.

Our students aren't getting enough sleep. In a survey run by our health class, 640 of the 890 students who responded, 72 percent, said they sleep less than seven hours on school nights. That isn't laziness. During adolescence, the body clock shifts later, so most teenagers can't easily fall asleep early. A 7:25 bell has many of them on a bus before sunrise, when their bodies still think it's the middle of the night.

That's why the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that high schools start no earlier than 8:30. This isn't a fringe idea. It's the standard pediatricians have set for adolescent health.

We don't have to guess what happens when a district follows it. [District], two counties over, moved to 8:30 three years ago. First-period tardies there dropped 31 percent, and their superintendent told our board that attendance rose by about a point.

That matters for more than morning grogginess. Our four-year graduation rate is 84 percent, which means roughly one in six of our students doesn't finish on time. Students rarely fall off track all at once. It happens a class at a time: missed first periods, a failed first-period course, absences that pile up until catching up feels impossible. Attendance is one of the earliest warning signs schools watch for. A change that gets more students to class, and awake, goes straight at that problem.

I can't promise the board a specific bump in our graduation rate, and no one honestly could. But I know that students who are rested show up more, learn more and end up in my office less. Those are the building blocks of finishing school.

The concerns about this change are real, and they deserve real answers.

Athletics: From November on, practices would end after dark, and away games would pull athletes out of last period. Those are solvable scheduling problems, through earlier practice slots, indoor sessions or working with our conference on game times. Our athletic director and coaches should help design the fix.

Working parents: Many families drop students off on the way to 8 a.m. shifts. The district can open the building early with a supervised space, such as the library, cafeteria or a study hall, so no student is left waiting outside.

Cost: The district estimates the new bus contract would add $180,000 a year. That's real money, and the board is right to weigh it. But it should also weigh what the current schedule costs us in lost class time, failed courses and students who fall behind.

I don't take these trade-offs lightly. But I see the price of the current schedule every morning, one tired student at a time. Our teenagers' biology isn't going to change to fit our bell schedule. We can change the bell schedule to fit them.

I urge the board to vote yes on April 14, and I urge parents and neighbors to come to the meeting and say so.

*[Name] is the school nurse at [High School].*

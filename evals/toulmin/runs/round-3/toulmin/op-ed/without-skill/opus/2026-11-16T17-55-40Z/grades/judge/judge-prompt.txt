<document>
**What I See in My Office at 7:30 in the Morning**

Every school morning, before most of this town has finished its coffee, my office starts filling up.

I'm the nurse at [School Name] High School. Last fall I logged 412 visits during first period alone: headaches, stomachaches, students who had nodded off at their desks and couldn't shake it. That's more than second period (198) and third period (171) combined. By mid-morning, the line at my door thins out. The students haven't changed. The clock has.

On April 14, the school board will vote on moving our start time from 7:25 to 8:30. I'm asking board members to vote yes.

What I see isn't a mystery. During adolescence, teenagers' body clocks shift later, and most can't fall asleep early even when they try. That's why the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that high schools start no earlier than 8:30. Our students show what happens when we ignore that advice. In a survey run by our health class, 640 of the 890 students who responded, 72 percent, said they sleep less than seven hours on school nights.

Sleep-deprived teenagers don't just feel lousy. They show up late, miss school, and struggle to focus in the classes they do attend. Over four years, those lost mornings add up to failed courses and missing credits, which are exactly what keeps a student from crossing the stage.

Our four-year graduation rate is 84 percent. That means roughly one in six of our students doesn't finish on time. I won't pretend a later bell will fix that by itself, because graduation depends on many things a start time can't touch. But educators have long treated poor attendance as one of the earliest warning signs that a student is at risk of not graduating. A later start goes straight at that problem.

We don't have to guess how this plays out. A district two counties over moved to an 8:30 start three years ago. Their first-period tardies dropped 31 percent, and their superintendent told our board that attendance rose by about a point. That isn't a miracle. It's a lot more students in their seats, and each of those school days is a chance to keep a kid on track.

The concerns about this change are real, and I don't want to wave them away.

Our athletic director points out that from November on, practices would end after dark, and away games would pull athletes out of last-period classes. Those are real scheduling problems, and they deserve real solutions, such as adjusted practice times, lighting, and a plan for making up missed class time on travel days. But they affect some students for part of the year. The first-period problem affects nearly all of them, every morning.

Many parents drop their kids off on the way to 8 a.m. shifts. The district could open the building early with a supervised space, like the library or cafeteria, so no student is left waiting in a parking lot and no parent has to choose between their job and their child's school day.

Then there's the money. The district estimates the new bus contract would cost an extra $180,000 a year. That is a serious number, and the board is right to weigh it. But it should be weighed against what we're already losing: lessons that never sink in at 7:25, and students who slide from tired to struggling to gone.

I see these kids every morning. They aren't lazy and they aren't faking. They're exhausted, for reasons that are well understood. We know what the pediatricians recommend, and we've watched a neighboring district make it work.

On April 14, I hope the board gives our students the extra hour.

*[Your Name], RN, is the school nurse at [School Name] High School. The views expressed here are the author's own.*

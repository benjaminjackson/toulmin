<document>
**Let our students sleep: Vote yes on an 8:30 start**

Every school morning, first period sends me a line of students. Last fall I logged 412 visits to my office during first period alone: headaches, stomachaches, and kids who had fallen asleep at their desks. That's more than second period (198) and third period (171) put together.

I'm the school nurse at [School Name] High School. On April 14, our school board will vote on moving the high school start time from 7:25 to 8:30. I'm asking the board to vote yes.

What I see in my office matches what students say. In a survey our health class ran, 640 of the 890 students who answered (72 percent) said they sleep less than seven hours on school nights. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that high schools start no earlier than 8:30. Right now we start more than an hour before that.

We don't have to guess what happens when a district takes that advice. A district two counties over moved to 8:30 three years ago. Their first-period tardies dropped 31 percent, and their superintendent told our board that attendance went up "about a point."

That matters for a number every board member should care about. Our four-year graduation rate is 84 percent, which means about one in six of our students doesn't finish on time. Students graduate by passing classes, and they can't pass classes they show up late to, miss, or sleep through. First period is where our students lose the most ground, and the later start is the one proposal before the board aimed right at it.

I won't promise that an 8:30 start will raise our graduation rate. Nobody can honestly promise that yet. The other district's first class to spend all four years on the later schedule hasn't graduated. When those results come in, our board should ask for them. But the early signs there are good, and a later start has already helped with the basic steps between a sleepy ninth grader and a diploma: showing up, showing up on time, and staying awake.

The change has real costs, and the board should weigh them honestly.

The district says the bus contract would cost an extra $180,000 a year. That's real money, and the board should be clear about where it would come from.

Our athletic director is right that from November on, practices would end after dark, and away games would pull athletes out of last-period classes. That is a real loss for those students. Here is the trade: some athletes would miss last period on away-game days. In return, every student would start every day in better shape for first period than they do now.

Many parents drop their kids off on the way to 8 a.m. shifts. An 8:30 start would leave some students arriving early or needing another ride. Those families need a plan before a new schedule begins. The district two counties over faced these same questions three years ago, and our board should ask how they solved them.

None of these problems is small. But saying no has a cost too, even if it never shows up as a line in the budget. If the board votes no, we keep the schedule that sent 412 students to my office during first period last fall. We keep asking nearly three out of four students to start the day short on sleep. And we keep hoping that a student who is late, absent or asleep in first period will somehow still make it to graduation.

I see these students before most adults in the building do. Many of them aren't sick. They're exhausted, and a later start is something this board can actually do about it.

On April 14, I hope the board votes yes on an 8:30 start.

*[Your Name] is the school nurse at [School Name] High School.*

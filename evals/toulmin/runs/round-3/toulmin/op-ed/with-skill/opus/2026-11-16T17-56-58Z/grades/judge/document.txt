**Our students are asleep at 7:25. Let them start at 8:30.**

On April 14, the school board will vote on whether to move the high school's start time from 7:25 to 8:30. As the school nurse, I see what 7:25 does to our students every morning, and I'm asking the board to vote yes.

Last fall I logged 412 visits to my office during first period. Headaches. Stomach complaints. Students who had fallen asleep at their desks and couldn't stay awake. That's more than second and third period combined: 198 and 171, or 369 together. First period is the busiest hour in my office, and it isn't close.

The students are telling us why. In a survey run by our health class, 640 of the 890 students who answered said they sleep less than seven hours on a school night. That's nearly three out of four. It's also why the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that high schools start no earlier than 8:30.

We don't have to guess what happens when a district makes this change. A district two counties over moved to 8:30 three years ago. Their first-period tardies dropped 31 percent, and their superintendent told our board that attendance went up "about a point." That means more students in their seats when the bell rings.

Why does this matter beyond my office? Our four-year graduation rate is 84 percent. Roughly one student in six doesn't finish on time. I won't claim a bell schedule alone will change that number. But students graduate by passing classes, and they pass classes by being in the room and awake for them. Right now, for too many of our kids, first period is a class they sleep through, arrive late to, or spend in my office. A later start goes straight at that problem.

The concerns on the other side are real, and the board should weigh them honestly.

The bus contract would cost an extra $180,000 a year. That is real money. The board should set it against what it buys: a first period that every high school student can actually use, every school day, all year.

The athletic director says practices would end after dark from November on, and that away games would pull athletes out of last-period classes. Both are true. But look at the trade we're already making. Missing last period for an away game affects some athletes on some days. Starting class short on sleep affects nearly three in four of the students who answered our survey, every school day.

Many parents drop their kids off on the way to 8 a.m. shifts, and a later start would leave those families with a gap. That is a problem to solve, not a reason to keep a schedule that isn't working. If the board votes yes, the district should come back with a plan for those students before the change takes effect.

Doing nothing isn't free either. Keeping 7:25 means another year of what I've described: a first period that sends more students to the nurse than the next two periods put together, and a school where most kids are running short on sleep.

I'm not a researcher. I'm the person students come to when they can't get through the first hour of the day. The board has evidence from our own school, from a nearby district that has already made this change, and from the nation's pediatricians, and all of it points the same way. On April 14, I hope they'll vote to let our students start at 8:30.

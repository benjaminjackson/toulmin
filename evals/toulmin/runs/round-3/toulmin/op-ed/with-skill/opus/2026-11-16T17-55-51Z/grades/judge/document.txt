**Our high school starts too early. On April 14, the board can fix that.**

Every school morning I see what a 7:25 start does to our students.

Last fall I logged 412 visits to my office during first period: headaches, stomachaches, kids who could not keep their eyes open. Second period brought 198. Third brought 171. First period alone sent me more students than the next two periods combined, and more than twice as many as either one.

These are not kids gaming the system. They are kids short on sleep. When our health class surveyed students, 640 of the 890 who answered, nearly three in four, said they sleep less than seven hours on a school night. Teenagers' body clocks run later than adults' do, which is why the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that high schools start no earlier than 8:30. We start more than an hour before that.

On April 14 the school board votes on moving our start time to 8:30. I am asking board members to vote yes.

We don't have to guess what happens next. A district two counties over made the same change three years ago. Their first-period tardies fell 31 percent, and their superintendent told our board that attendance went up "about a point."

That matters for the number this community cares about most: our four-year graduation rate, which stands at 84 percent. About one student in six does not finish on time. Students rarely fall off track in a single day. They fall behind one missed first period, one class slept through, one morning on a cot in my office at a time. A later start will not close that gap on its own, and I won't claim the neighboring district has proved it will. But it goes straight at the first step toward a diploma: being in class, and awake, when the day begins.

The objections to this change are real, and they deserve a straight answer.

Our athletic director is right that from November on, practices would end after dark, and that away games would pull athletes out of last period. Many parents drop their kids off on the way to 8 a.m. shifts, and a later start makes those mornings harder. The district estimates the new bus contract would cost $180,000 a year.

Those costs land on some students and some families, some of the time. The cost of the schedule we have now lands on most of our students every morning, and athletes are in those hallways at 7:25 too. Missing last period for an away game is a problem. Starting every school day short on sleep is a bigger one. Families who depend on an early drop-off deserve a plan, and before it votes, the board should ask the district exactly what it will offer them.

As for the $180,000: it is real money in a tight budget, and the board will have to decide whether it is worth spending. Here is what I would weigh it against: 412 first-period trips to my office in a single fall, each one a class a student wasn't in.

I'd also ask the board to do more than vote. Commit to measuring the results. Report first-period tardies, attendance and nurse visits after the first year, and report the graduation rate when the first class to spend four full years on the later schedule walks across the stage. If those numbers don't move, the board will know it, and so will the rest of us. If they do, we will have done right by every student who now spends first period with their head on a desk or in a chair outside my door.

I see our students at their most tired, before most adults in this building see them at all. Seven twenty-five is too early. On April 14, I hope the board agrees.

*[Name] is the school nurse at [School].*

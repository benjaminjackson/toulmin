**My busiest hour is the first one. A later start can change that.**

Every school day last fall, the busiest hour in my office was the first one.

I'm the nurse at [School Name] High School, and I keep a log. From September through December, I recorded 412 visits during first period: headaches, stomachaches, and students who simply could not keep their eyes open. Second period brought 198 visits. Third brought 171. First period outpaced the next two combined.

I don't think that's a coincidence. Our school day starts at 7:25. For many students, that means a bus before 6:45 and an alarm well before that. On April 14, the school board will vote on moving the start to 8:30. I urge the board to vote yes.

The science here isn't in dispute. During adolescence, teenagers' internal clocks shift later, and many can't fall asleep before 11 p.m. no matter how responsible they are. Teens need eight to ten hours of sleep a night. That's why the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that high schools start no earlier than 8:30.

Our own students show how far short they fall. In a survey run by our health class, 640 of the 890 students who responded, more than seven in ten, said they sleep less than seven hours on school nights.

A sleep-deprived teenager is not a lazy teenager. Sleep loss shows up as the complaints I see every morning, and as poor concentration, irritability and low mood. It also shows up in whether kids make it to class at all.

We don't have to guess what happens when a district makes this change. [District Name], two counties over, moved its high school start to 8:30 three years ago. First-period tardies dropped 31 percent. Its superintendent told our board that attendance rose by about a point.

That matters most for one number: our four-year graduation rate of 84 percent. One in six of our students doesn't finish on time. A diploma isn't earned on a single day. It's built over hundreds of mornings of showing up, staying awake and keeping up. A start time won't fix everything that stands between a student and graduation. But it would remove an obstacle we put there ourselves. I ask the board to adopt the change and then track graduation, attendance and nurse visits, so this community can see the results for itself.

The concerns about this change are real, and I don't dismiss them.

**Athletics.** Our athletic director is right that from November on, practices would end after dark, and away games would pull athletes out of last period. Other districts that have switched have had to rework practice times, facilities and game schedules. Ours can too, and the board should ask for that plan along with the vote.

**Working families.** Many parents drop their kids off on the way to 8 a.m. shifts. A later start shouldn't leave those students waiting on the curb. Opening the building early, with supervised space in the library or cafeteria, would let those families keep their routines. It would also give students a calm place to start the day.

**Cost.** The district estimates the new bus contract would cost an extra $180,000 a year. That is real money, and it deserves scrutiny. It should also be weighed against what we lose now, when hundreds of students spend their first class of the day exhausted, late or in my office instead of learning.

I see these students before most adults in the building do. I know who's slumped over, who skipped breakfast and who was up past midnight and still caught the 6:40 bus. Teenagers can't change their biology. Our school board can change the schedule.

On April 14, I hope the board chooses a start time that fits the students we actually have.

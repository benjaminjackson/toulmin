**What I see during first period: why our board should vote for an 8:30 start**

Every school day, shortly after the first bell, the line starts forming outside my office.

Last fall I logged 412 visits during first period alone: headaches, stomachaches, students who had fallen asleep at their desks and couldn't shake it off. That is more than second period (198) and third period (171) combined. By mid-morning, many of those same students are fine. They weren't sick. They were exhausted.

On April 14, the school board will vote on whether to move our high school start time from 7:25 to 8:30. As the person who sees the cost of the current schedule up close, I'm asking the board to vote yes.

This isn't about teenagers being lazy. During adolescence, the body's internal clock shifts later, and most teens can't fall asleep much before 11 p.m., however early they go to bed. A student who has to catch a bus before 7 a.m. is working against their own biology. That is why the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that high schools start no earlier than 8:30.

Our students' own answers back this up. In a survey run by our health class, 640 of the 890 students who responded, nearly three in four, said they sleep fewer than seven hours on school nights. Teenagers need eight to ten.

Losing sleep night after night does more than make kids yawn. It is linked to weaker concentration and memory, more anxiety and depression, and more car crashes among young drivers. It also shows up in whether students are in class at all.

We don't have to guess what happens when a district makes this change. A district two counties over moved to 8:30 three years ago. Its first-period tardies fell 31 percent, and its superintendent told our board that attendance rose by about a percentage point.

That matters for graduation. Our four-year graduation rate is 84 percent, which means roughly one in six of our students doesn't finish on time. Students rarely fall off track all at once. It starts with missed first periods, then a failed class, then credits that are hard to make up. That's why attendance is one of the earliest warning signs schools watch. No one can promise that a later start alone will raise our graduation rate by a set number. But a schedule that gets more students into their seats, awake and ready to learn, is one of the most direct tools this board has to help the students closest to slipping.

The concerns about this change are real, and they deserve real answers.

**Athletics.** Our athletic director is right that practices would end after dark from November on, and that away games would pull some athletes out of last period. Lighted fields, indoor winter practices and adjusted game times can help. And missing part of one class for a game costs less than starting every day short on sleep.

**Working parents.** Many families drop their kids off on the way to 8 a.m. shifts. The school could open early, with a supervised library and cafeteria, so students have a safe place to eat breakfast, study or simply rest.

**Cost.** The district estimates the new bus contract would cost an extra $180,000 a year. That is real money, and the board should weigh it seriously. But students who fall behind are expensive too, through credit recovery, summer school and extra support. Helping students stay on track from the start is a better use of our dollars than paying to catch them up later.

Every morning I send tired students back to class after an ice pack or a few minutes' rest, knowing that what they really need is sleep. On April 14, the board can give it to them. I hope it will.

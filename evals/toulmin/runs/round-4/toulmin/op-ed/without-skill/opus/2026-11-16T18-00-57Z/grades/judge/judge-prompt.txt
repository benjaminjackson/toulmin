<document>
**Let Our Teenagers Sleep: Move the High School Bell to 8:30**

Every school morning, my office fills up before most adults have finished their coffee.

I'm the nurse at [School Name] High School. Last fall I logged every student visit, and the pattern was hard to miss. During first period alone, I recorded 412 visits for headaches, stomachaches and kids who couldn't keep their heads up at their desks. That's more than second period (198) and third period (171) combined.

These aren't kids gaming the system. They are kids who are exhausted.

When our health classes surveyed students, 640 of the 890 who responded (about 72 percent) said they sleep less than seven hours on a school night. Teenagers need eight to 10. That isn't laziness. In adolescence, the body's internal clock shifts later, and most teens can't fall asleep early no matter when the lights go out. A 7:25 bell asks them to be awake, alert and learning at an hour when their bodies are still telling them to sleep.

That's why the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that middle and high schools start no earlier than 8:30 a.m. On April 14, our school board will vote on doing exactly that. I urge them to vote yes.

We don't have to guess what happens next. A district two counties over moved its high school start to 8:30 three years ago. Its first-period tardies fell 31 percent, and its superintendent told our board that attendance rose by about a point.

That matters for more than morning routines. Students who miss class fall behind, and students who fall behind are the ones most at risk of not finishing. Our four-year graduation rate is 84 percent, which means roughly one in six of our students doesn't graduate on time. I won't promise that a later bell will change that number on its own, and I'd be wary of anyone who did. But showing up, awake and ready to learn, is where finishing school starts. Every student who makes it to first period instead of to my office is a step toward the stage.

Now the hard part. The concerns about this change are real, and I've heard them.

Our athletic director points out that from November on, practices would end after dark, and away games would pull athletes out of last-period classes. Many parents drop their kids off on the way to 8 a.m. shifts and can't simply leave later. And the district estimates the new bus contract would cost an extra $180,000 a year.

I don't dismiss any of that. $180,000 is real money in a school budget. But these are logistics problems, and logistics problems have solutions, some of which a district down the road has already found. Before the vote, I'd ask the board to put direct questions to that district. How did you handle athletics and travel? What did you offer families who need an early drop-off: an open library, a supervised breakfast, a morning study hall? What did transportation actually end up costing?

What we can't schedule around is biology. I see the results of our current schedule every morning: a line of tired kids at my door, losing class time they can't afford to lose. The medical guidance is clear, and a neighboring district has shown us it can work.

On April 14, I hope the board will give our students the extra hour. They'll use it well.

*[Your Name] is the school nurse at [School Name] High School.*

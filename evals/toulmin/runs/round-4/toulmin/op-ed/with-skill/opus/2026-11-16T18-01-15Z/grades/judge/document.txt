**Give our students back the hour first period is costing them**

I am the nurse at [School Name] High School, and I can tell what time it is by who comes through my door.

Last fall I logged 412 visits to my office during first period: headaches, stomachaches, students who fell asleep at their desks and couldn't shake it off. That is more than second period (198) and third period (171) put together. The same students in the same building come to me about half as often once the morning is an hour older.

The likeliest reason is sleep. When our health class surveyed students, 640 of the 890 who answered, nearly three in four, said they sleep less than seven hours on a school night. Teenagers' internal clocks shift later during adolescence, so this isn't a discipline problem. That is why the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that high schools start no earlier than 8:30. On April 14, our school board will vote on moving our start time from 7:25 to exactly that. It should vote yes.

The fair question is whether moving the bell changes anything beyond how rested students feel. We don't have to guess. A district two counties over moved to 8:30 three years ago. Their first-period tardies fell 31 percent, and their superintendent told our board that attendance went up about a point.

Those are the numbers that lead to graduation. Our four-year graduation rate is 84 percent, which means about one student in six doesn't finish on time. Students rarely fall off track in a single day. They fall behind one missed, late or slept-through morning at a time, and here, first period is where most of those mornings are lost.

I won't promise the board a specific graduation number. No honest person can. I am asking the board to make the change and hold it to account. Report to the public each year on first-period tardies, attendance, nurse visits and course failures, and on graduation as the first classes under the new schedule finish. If tardies and attendance haven't improved after two full years, the board should say so plainly and reconsider. On those two measures, our neighbors have already passed that test.

The objections are real, and they deserve answers.

Cost: the district says the new bus contract would add $180,000 a year. At least 890 students attend our high school, since that's how many answered the survey. That makes the cost at most about $200 per student per year, or roughly a dollar a school day.

Working parents: many families drop students off on the way to 8 a.m. shifts. I propose the district keep the building open and supervised from 7:25, in the cafeteria or library, so no parent has to choose between their job and their child's school day.

Athletics: our athletic director is right that practices would end after dark from November on and that away games would pull athletes out of last period. That is a real trade, and I would make it. It affects some students on some afternoons for part of the year. Our first-period problem affects every student, every morning, all year. The board should approve the change and have the athletic schedule worked out with the athletic director before it takes effect, instead of letting the practice calendar decide for every student.

And if the board says no? Next fall looks like last fall: another 400-plus first-period visits to my office, another year of three-quarters of our students running short on sleep, and the same 84 percent.

I see these students first thing every morning. Give them the hour. Vote yes on April 14.

*[Name] is the school nurse at [School Name] High School.*

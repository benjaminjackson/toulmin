**I see what 7:25 does to our students, every morning**

Every school day, the busiest hour in my office is the first one.

Last fall I logged 412 student visits during first period. They came in with headaches and stomachaches, and some had fallen asleep at their desks. That's more than second period (198) and third period (171) put together. A couple of hours into the day, the line at my door has dropped by more than half.

I'm the school nurse at [School Name]. On April 14 our school board will vote on moving the high school start time from 7:25 to 8:30. I'm asking them to vote yes.

Our students are tired, and they tell us so. When our health class surveyed students, 640 of the 890 who answered said they sleep less than seven hours on school nights. That's nearly three in four. This isn't a few students staying up too late. It's most of our school. The American Academy of Pediatrics, the main professional body of the doctors who care for our children, recommends that high schools start no earlier than 8:30. Right now we start more than an hour earlier than that.

We don't have to guess what happens when a district changes. A district two counties over moved to 8:30 three years ago. Their first-period tardies fell by 31 percent. Their superintendent told our board that attendance went up by about a point.

Why does this matter? Our four-year graduation rate is 84 percent. That means about one student in six doesn't finish on time. I won't promise you that a later bell fixes that by itself. The district that made the switch hasn't yet had a class go through all four years on the new schedule, so nobody can honestly say yet what it does to graduation. But a student who is in class and awake has a better chance of passing that class, and passing classes, one after another, is how students graduate. A student asleep in my office at 7:50 is not passing first period.

The costs of changing are real, and the board should hear them plainly.

The district estimates the new bus contract would cost an extra $180,000 a year. That's real money. But the current schedule isn't free either. We pay for it in 412 trips out of first period, in students who sit through class without taking it in, and in tardies that a neighbouring district has already shown can be cut by almost a third. Keeping 7:25 means accepting all of that again next year.

Our athletic director has raised fair concerns. From November on, practices would end after dark, and away games would pull athletes out of last-period classes. Those students matter to me too, and they're also among the students sitting in first period now. The district two counties over has been through three seasons on the later schedule. Before the vote, I'd ask our board and athletic director to find out how their teams handled practice times and travel, and to bring that answer to the community.

Many parents drop their children off on their way to 8 a.m. shifts. A later start shouldn't punish them for having jobs. I'd ask the district to look at opening the building early, with supervision, so students can wait somewhere safe and warm instead of in a parking lot.

Finally, I'd ask the board to do one more thing alongside a yes vote: measure the results. Track first-period tardies, attendance, nurse visits and the graduation rate, and report them to the public every year. I'll keep my log the same way I kept it this year, so we can compare. If the later start works, we'll see it. If it doesn't, we'll know that too.

I've spent enough mornings handing out ice packs and waking students up to know that first period is where many of our students start falling behind. On April 14, our board can give them the hour of sleep they need. I hope they will.

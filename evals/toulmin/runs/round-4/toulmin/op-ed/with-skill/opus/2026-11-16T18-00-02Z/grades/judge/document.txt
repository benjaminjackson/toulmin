**Our teenagers need sleep. The school board can give it to them on April 14.**

Every school morning, my office fills up before most adults have finished their coffee. Last fall I logged 412 student visits during first period alone: headaches, stomachaches, kids who had fallen asleep at their desks and couldn't shake it. That is more than second and third period combined, which saw 198 and 171.

I'm the school nurse at [School Name]. On April 14, our school board votes on moving the high school start time from 7:25 to 8:30. I'm asking board members to vote yes, and I'm asking you to tell them you agree.

What I see in my office is no mystery. When our health class surveyed students, 640 of the 890 who answered, nearly three in four, said they sleep less than seven hours on a school night. Teenagers' internal clocks shift later during adolescence, so telling them to go to bed earlier doesn't fix a 7:25 start. That is why the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that high schools start no earlier than 8:30. We start an hour and five minutes earlier than that.

We don't have to guess what a later start does. A district two counties over moved to 8:30 three years ago. Their first-period tardies dropped 31 percent, and their superintendent told our board that attendance rose about a point.

That matters for the number this community should care about most. Our four-year graduation rate is 84 percent, which means roughly one in six of our students doesn't finish on time. I won't promise that a later bell alone will close that gap. Nobody can honestly promise that. But I know what a student needs to graduate: to be in class, and awake enough to learn there. Every student who starts the day in my office, or doesn't make it to school at all, falls a little further behind. Getting more students to first period, on time and alert, is where a higher graduation rate starts.

So I'd ask the board to do more than vote yes. Commit to reporting, every year, first-period tardies, attendance, nurse visits and the graduation rate, so all of us can see whether the change is working. If those numbers don't move, we should know it.

The objections are real, and I've heard them. Our athletic director points out that from November on, practices would end after dark, and away games would pull athletes out of last-period classes. Many parents drop their kids off on the way to 8 a.m. shifts. And the district estimates the new bus contract will cost an extra $180,000 a year.

These deserve answers, not dismissal. A yes vote should come with a plan: a supervised space open early for students whose parents drop them off on the way to work; a review of practice and game schedules with coaches before next season, so athletes miss as little class as possible; and a clear accounting of where the $180,000 will come from.

That is real money. But the current schedule has costs too. They just don't show up in the budget. They show up in my office: 412 first-period visits in a single fall, three-quarters of our students short on sleep, and one in six not graduating on time. Voting no doesn't make those costs go away. It means we keep paying them, every year.

I'd like to see a shorter line outside my door at the start of the school day. Please contact board members before April 14 at [contact information], or come to the meeting, and ask them to vote yes on 8:30.

*[Name] is the school nurse at [School Name].*

The brief between the <brief> tags is what the writer was asked to do and shows the facts the writer had. You, as the reader, have not seen the brief. Use it only to check what the document does with those facts.

<reader>
The hotel's general manager, who decides who becomes front office manager. He likes the writer but is also interviewing an outside candidate who ran the front desk at a larger hotel, and he believes the rise in guest scores came mostly from the renovation.
</reader>

<brief>
Help me write a case for my promotion. I'm the assistant front office manager at a 180-room hotel, and the front office manager job is open. I'm applying, and my general manager wants it in writing by Friday.

Facts:
- I've worked here 6 years, the last 2 as assistant manager.
- Our guest score for check-in on the main review site went from 7.9 to 8.6 over the last 18 months. The lobby and all the rooms were renovated during the same time.
- I rewrote the front desk training. New hires now work the desk alone after 5 shifts instead of 9.
- Front desk turnover was 9 of 14 people in the year before I became assistant manager, and 5 of 14 last year.
- I've covered the front office manager's job alone for the 7 weeks since she left.
- I have never managed a budget. The front office manager owns a $1.1 million payroll budget.

I want it to say I'm the main reason our guest scores went up. One page.
</brief>



<document>
**To:** [General Manager]
**From:** [Your name], Assistant Front Office Manager
**Date:** Friday, November 20, 2026
**Re:** My application for Front Office Manager

I'm applying for the Front Office Manager position. For the past seven weeks I've done the job alone, and the front desk has kept running without a gap. I'd like to keep doing it with the title and the accountability that come with it.

**I have built a front desk team that stays.** In the year before I became assistant manager, 9 of our 14 front desk staff left (64%). Last year only 5 of 14 left (36%). That's four fewer people walking out the door each year, with all the vacancies, overtime and lost experience that each departure brings.

**I made new hires useful faster.** I rewrote our front desk training. New hires now work the desk alone after 5 shifts instead of 9. Together with lower turnover, that cuts our paid training time from about 81 shifts a year to about 25.

**Guests have noticed.** Our check-in score on [review site] rose from 7.9 to 8.6 over the last 18 months. The renovation gave guests a better lobby and better rooms, and it deserves real credit for that. But check-in is the moment a guest meets our people. The staff who greeted guests during those 18 months were hired, trained and kept by the system I built.

**I know the property and the team.** I've worked here six years, the last two as assistant manager. For seven weeks I've run the front office alone. The team already works for me, and nothing has had to wait for a new manager to learn the hotel.

**On the budget:** I haven't yet owned a budget the size of the front office's $1.1 million payroll, and I won't pretend otherwise. But the results above are payroll results: less hiring, less training time and less overtime to cover vacancies. I'd like to formalize what I've been doing informally. For my first two quarters I propose a monthly payroll review with you [or the controller], set against budget, until you're satisfied I'm managing it well.

If the role goes to an outside hire, that person will need months to learn our systems, our guests and a team that is stable for the first time in years. I can keep that team and those results going from day one.

I'd welcome the chance to discuss this with you.

[Your name]
</document>

Score the document on the seven items below, each from 0 to 10, and give each score one sentence of reason.

1. agree: would this reader, including the one who resists, come to agree or act as the writer wants?
2. honest: no claim is stated more strongly than the facts in the brief carry, and no fact or number is invented.
3. works_the_facts: figures are derived from what the brief gave (rates, totals, the pace a target needs against the pace already reached, what happens if nothing changes), and each is put in its most favourable true form.
4. right_comparison: the alternative to what is proposed is described as the facts show it, including any trend.
5. qualification: any condition or hedge names something that could turn out either way and that the reader can check, with a date, a number and a consequence if it is missed. Give a score of null when the document needs no qualification.
6. objections: the resisting reader's objection is met, and no objection is raised that the facts in the brief already answer.
7. fit: the document has the form and length the brief asked for.

Use the whole scale from 0 to 10. A 5 is an ordinary competent draft.

I'm going to speak at our county planning commission tonight in favor of a housing project, and I want help writing what I'll say. The church I belong to wants to build 64 apartments on its back parking lot. All of them would rent to households earning under 60% of the area median income.

Facts:

- The county's own housing report says we are short about 2,100 affordable homes.
- The traffic study estimates 310 more car trips a day on Alder Road, and says the intersection at Alder and 4th would drop from a C rating to a D at rush hour.
- The plan has 72 parking spaces for the 64 units.

Each speaker gets 3 minutes.

<document>
Good evening, Commissioners. My name is [Your Name]. I've lived on Alder Road for nineteen years, two blocks from [Church Name], and I'm here to ask you to approve these 64 apartments.

First, the traffic, because it's a fair concern and I share it. The traffic study estimates 310 more trips a day on Alder Road. It says the intersection at Alder and 4th would drop from a C to a D at rush hour. I drive through that intersection. I'll be one of the people waiting a little longer at that light. I'm not going to pretend that cost is zero.

But a D is not a failing grade. It means a slower rush hour, not a broken intersection. And I'd ask you to weigh a few extra seconds at a stoplight against what's on the other side of the scale.

This county's own housing report says we are short about 2,100 affordable homes. Right now, 412 households are on the housing authority's waiting list, and the average wait is three years. That's three years of families doubling up, of seniors choosing between rent and medicine, of people who work here commuting in from somewhere cheaper. Some of those commuters are already driving on Alder Road.

Every one of these 64 apartments would rent to a household earning under 60 percent of the area median income. That is exactly the kind of household on that waiting list. Sixty-four homes won't close a gap of 2,100. But it's a real share of the problem, and it's 64 more homes than we'll have if we say no.

And look at where they'd go. The church's back parking lot is 1.8 acres of asphalt that sits empty six days a week. We wouldn't be clearing open space or tearing anything down. We'd be taking land that's already paved and putting it to work every day of the week instead of one.

Seventy-two parking spaces for 64 units means residents will park on the site, not on our street.

I've watched this neighborhood for nineteen years. The people who would live here are the people who already work in our stores, care for our parents, and teach our kids. They should be able to live here too.

When we ask where affordable housing should go, the answer is usually "somewhere else." Tonight we have a site, a willing owner, and a plan. I'm a neighbor, and I'm asking you to say yes.

Thank you.

Score the document on the seven items below, each from 0 to 10, and give each score one sentence of reason.

1. agree: would this reader, including the one who resists, come to agree or act as the writer wants?
2. honest: no claim is stated more strongly than the facts in the brief carry, and no fact or number is invented.
3. works_the_facts: figures are derived from what the brief gave (rates, totals, the pace a target needs against the pace already reached, what happens if nothing changes), and each is put in its most favourable true form.
4. right_comparison: the alternative to what is proposed is described as the facts show it, including any trend.
5. qualification: any condition or hedge names something that could turn out either way and that the reader can check, with a date, a number and a consequence if it is missed. Give a score of null when the document needs no qualification.
6. objections: the resisting reader's objection is met, and no objection is raised that the facts in the brief already answer.
7. fit: the document has the form and length the brief asked for.

Use the whole scale from 0 to 10. A 5 is an ordinary competent draft.

Good evening, commissioners. I've lived on Alder Road for 19 years, two blocks from the church that's applying tonight. I'm also a member of that church, so you should know that up front. But I'm speaking to you as a neighbor, because neighbors are the ones who will live with this project, traffic and all.

This project is 64 apartments, every one of them for households earning under 60 percent of the area median income. That's enough homes for about one in six of the households on that waiting list. And it puts them on 1.8 acres of pavement that sits empty six days a week. Nobody loses a home, a business, or a park. A parking lot becomes a place to live.

I want to talk about traffic directly, because I drive Alder Road every day and I've read the study. It estimates 310 more car trips a day on Alder, and it says the intersection at Alder and 4th will drop from a C to a D at rush hour. I'm not going to tell you that's nothing. It means a longer wait at that intersection at rush hour, and I'll be one of the people waiting. But a D is a slower intersection, not a failing one, and it's at rush hour, not all day.

On parking: the plan provides 72 spaces for 64 apartments. That's more than one space per home, so this project isn't counting on the street to absorb its residents' cars.

So here is the trade in front of you. On one side, a slower rush hour at one intersection for those of us who live on Alder. On the other, 64 households who would otherwise spend years on a waiting list. I live on that road, I'll pay my share of that cost, and I'd make that trade.

If you say no tonight, that lot stays empty six days a week, the shortage in your own report doesn't shrink by a single home, and those 412 households keep waiting.

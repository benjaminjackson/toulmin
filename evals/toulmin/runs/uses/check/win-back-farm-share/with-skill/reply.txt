It mostly works. It owns the failure plainly, doesn't make excuses, lists real changes and closes with a low-pressure reply option. But it has one gap a reader who left over the hail will notice right away, and a few smaller problems.

## The main weakness: it apologises for one thing and fixes another

The email names exactly what went wrong: "We didn't offer refunds or credit, and I sent only one short email about it." Then nothing on the list of changes deals with that. The second field and the hail nets make crop loss less likely. Pause credits are a vacation perk. The reader is left asking the obvious question: **"And if it happens again, what do I get?"** The email never answers it.

What upset these people wasn't the hail. It was paying full price for half boxes and being told almost nothing. To win them back, the email needs to say what happens to members next time a disaster hits. That could be a credit, a partial refund, or at least a promise to tell members what's happening. Only you can say what you'll commit to, but without it "you deserved a better response from me" is an apology with nothing behind it.

## Second weakness: no make-good for last year

"You paid for full boxes" admits you owe them something, and the next move is asking for $540 again. Some readers will notice that. Roughly, each household lost half of 6 of 22 weeks, about **$74 of produce**. A credit of that size on a returning share would cost you about $7,200 if all 97 came back. Unsold shares are worth $28,080 (52 × $540). Whether to offer it is your call, but it's the strongest lever you have, and the email currently leaves it out.

## Smaller problems

- **"I think I know why" assumes too much.** Some of the 97 moved, had too many vegetables, or left for other reasons. They'll feel misread. A light line like "If it was something else, I'd like to hear that too" covers them, and your closing reply invitation already half does this.
- **The fixes don't clearly connect to hail.** "Higher ground" sounds like a flood fix, and the nets cover only tomatoes and peppers. A sceptical reader will wonder how much of July 9 this would actually have prevented. If the new field is far enough away that one storm is less likely to hit both, say so. If it isn't, don't oversell it.
- **Pause credits are filed under "what we changed after the hail."** They're a good perk, but they don't belong there, and they make it look like you misunderstood the complaint. Mention them separately as something new.
- **"$31,000 cost us" can read as asking for sympathy.** It works best tied directly to the price: you spent it and didn't raise the price. The draft nearly does this, but the two sentences are split.
- **"Thank you for the seasons"** assumes everyone was a member for more than one season.

## Length

The draft is about 185 words without the subject line and signature, so it's close to your 200-word limit. Anything you add (a disaster policy, a credit) has to replace something. The pause-credit bullet and the "higher ground" explanation are the easiest places to cut.

**Bottom line:** the tone is right. Before sending, decide what you'll promise members if a disaster happens again, and whether you'll offer anything for last year. Those two answers matter more than any wording change.

## Will it work?

It probably gets read and might get a phone screen from a hiring manager who's open to career changers. It's clear, honest, and organized. But it reads like a teacher's letter sent to a plant, not a letter to someone who runs training in a GMP environment. Against applicants who already have GMP experience, it won't stand out. Its main argument is "good teachers make good trainers," and it assumes the reader already believes that. A pharma hiring manager may not. Their job is to keep the plant audit-ready, so they'll want the connection shown.

## The biggest problem: your main claim is missing

You asked for the letter to say you can cut their new-hire training time in half. The draft leaves that out and never tells you it did. Still, the writer had a point. Nothing in your facts supports that claim:

- You don't know how long their training takes now, or why.
- None of your facts show you shortening anyone's training. The 12-hour teacher training has no "before" number to compare against.
- In a GMP plant, much of the training time is fixed by the SOPs (written procedures) and the sign-off process. A hiring manager would likely read "cut it in half" as a sign you don't yet understand their world. That's the doubt you can least afford, since you're already short on regulated-industry experience.

So the choice is yours, but make it knowingly:

- **Bold version:** keep "cut it in half." It will stand out, but it invites the question "based on what?", and you can't answer that yet.
- **Narrower version the facts support:** say you'd measure how long new hires take to be signed off as able to work on their own, find where they get stuck, and shorten that. Your AP and safety work shows you work that way.
- **The middle option needs facts only you have.** Did your 12-hour program replace something longer? Do new teachers run labs alone sooner than they used to? If so, that's evidence for a version of the speed claim, and it belongs in the letter.

## Weak spots, in order of importance

1. **It's not written in the plant's language.** It never mentions SOPs, operators being qualified to work alone, retraining after an error, or audits. Your experience maps onto all of these. Training new teachers until they can run a lab alone is very close to qualifying a new operator. Writing lab procedures is very close to writing SOPs. The letter should make those links directly and not leave the reader to do it.

2. **The strongest evidence is in the wrong order.** The new-teacher training is the closest match to the job. Training adults is what a training coordinator does, so it should come first. The safety record comes second. The AP pass rate is the weakest of the three for this reader. It's about teenagers and exam scores, and a skeptic will ask whether the students changed rather than the teaching. Keep it, but shorten it and put it last.

3. **The safety record has no scale and no baseline.** "No injuries beyond first aid" means more if the reader knows how many people it covers. With about 140 of your own students a year since 2019, that's roughly 1,000 students. It's more if the program covers the whole school. Did injuries happen before 2019? If so, the before-and-after is your strongest single fact, and the letter should include it.

4. **The writer added details you never gave.** "Check that they can actually do the work, not just recall it," "I've kept detailed records for years," "tracking where students struggled and changing my teaching based on the data," and "holding people to the standard every day" all came from the writer. Some may be true. An interviewer will ask about each one, so keep only what you can back with a real example.

5. **The answer on the GMP gap is too thin.** Being upfront about it is right. But "I use Google Classroom, so I'll learn quickly" basically asks the reader to overlook the gap without giving them a reason. A concrete step would answer it: a GMP basics course you've started or finished, or a plan for your first 30 to 60 days. Only include what you'll actually do. The line about documentation being part of the work is good. Keep it.

6. **The opening and closing are generic.** Nothing in them is specific to this company. Two things go unsaid that the reader will wonder about:
   - Why you're leaving teaching. One positive sentence is enough.
   - That you live nearby and plan to stay. A 30-minute commute shows staying power, and that matters when a plant is training someone from outside the industry.

7. **Small point:** "140 students across five classes" sits in the results paragraph, where it doesn't prove anything. It shows how many people you handle, so it fits better with the safety or training points.

Worth keeping: the honest tone, the short paragraphs, and the fact that it fits on one page.

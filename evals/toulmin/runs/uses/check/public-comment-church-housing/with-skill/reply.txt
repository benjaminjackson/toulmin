Mostly, yes. The best thing in it is you: someone who lives two blocks away on the road that takes the traffic, standing up to say you'll accept that cost. Leading with traffic and not dodging the C-to-D drop is the right call, because the commissioners will believe you more for it. The facts are used accurately and it ends on a clear request. The problems are a few lines that claim more than your facts support, and those are the lines a commissioner or an opponent will pick at.

**Where it's weak, most important first**

1. **Sunday parking is the hole a commissioner will find.** The speech says the lot "sits empty six days a week," so it's full on the seventh. Then it says the 72 spaces mean the project "won't push cars onto neighborhood streets." But if the apartments go on the church's parking lot, where do Sunday worshippers park? If they end up on Alder Road, that sentence is false, and your neighbors will know it. You need the answer before tonight. Does the plan keep or share parking for the church, or move services, or does the church accept overflow? Whatever the answer is, put it in. If you don't know, cut "it won't push cars onto neighborhood streets" and keep only the plain fact: 72 spaces for 64 units.

2. **"It covers its own needs" is the writer's claim, not a fact you have.** 72 spaces is about 1.1 per unit. Whether that's enough depends on the county's parking rule for this kind of project. If 72 meets or beats that rule, say so. That's a point the commission can actually use. If you don't know, don't claim it's enough.

3. **The traffic answer explains a rating to people who already know what it means.** Commissioners know what a D means. The question they'll ask is whether D meets the county's own standard for that intersection. Many counties accept D at rush hour. If yours does, saying so is much stronger than "it doesn't mean gridlock." Also, "slightly longer wait" plays down the cost. If the study gives the added delay in seconds, use that number. If not, just say "a longer wait at rush hour." Don't shrink it, because your credibility here comes from not shrinking it.

4. **"My neighbors and I can handle a slower light."** You can only speak for yourself. If neighbors who oppose this are in the room, this line works against you. Change it to "I."

5. **Details you didn't give the writer:**
   - **"These are the people on that waiting list."** That may not be true. Housing authority waiting lists are often for vouchers or public housing, and those households can earn well below 60% of the area median income. Say these apartments serve the same kind of need, not that they serve those exact households.
   - **"Cashiers, home health aides, teachers' aides, seniors on fixed incomes."** These are illustrations, not facts about who would live there. They're probably harmless, but they're also the easiest thing to cut for time.
   - **"Some of the people who would live here already worship at this church."** Cut this one for sure. Besides being unsupported, it suggests church members would get first pick. Opponents could raise that as a fair-housing problem, and it could cause trouble with the project's funding. You don't want to hand them that tonight.

6. **Length.** It's about 440 words. At a nervous podium pace that runs a little past three minutes, and the part that gets cut off when the timer goes is the request. Trim about 50 words. "Here's what it offers," the list of jobs, and "We rarely find land like this" can all go without losing anything. Read it aloud with a timer at least once.

**What I'd leave alone:** the opening with your 19 years on Alder, taking on traffic first, using the county's own report for the 2,100 shortfall, "nobody is being displaced," and the close that weighs a slower light against a three-year wait. That last comparison is the whole argument, and it works.

**One thing to add if you can:** the commission has to justify its decision with reasons that fit its rules, not just with need. If the county's housing plan or comprehensive plan sets a goal for affordable housing or for using land that sits idle, one sentence saying this project meets that goal gives them a reason they can write down. Only add it if you know the goal exists.

**Mostly, yes.** The speech is written for the right audience. Planning commissioners hear a lot of "we need housing" testimony, and what they rarely hear is a neighbor on the affected street saying "I know the traffic cost, I drive it daily, and I accept it." The draft puts that first, and it's your strongest asset. The ending works too, because it brings the slower light back and sets it against the three-year wait.

There are some real weak spots, though. A few could cost you credibility in the room.

## Problems that could backfire

1. **You never say you're a member of the church.** It presents you only as a neighbor. If an opponent or a commissioner learns you belong to the applicant's church, your "neighbor who accepts the cost" standing falls apart and you look like you hid it. Say it in your first two sentences. Being open about it makes you more credible, and your 19 years on Alder is still the stronger credential.

2. **The parking argument has a hole.** "Sits empty 6 days a week" means it's full on the seventh. If the apartments take the back lot, where do Sunday worshippers park? Someone opposed will ask, and "it won't push cars onto neighborhood streets" will look wrong. Also, 72 spaces for 64 units is only about 1.1 per unit, which opponents may call under-parked. Before tonight, find out how the church plans to handle Sunday parking (a front lot, shared spaces, something else). Then either address it in one sentence or tone the parking claim down to what you can defend.

3. **"Some of the people who would live here already worship at this church."** This isn't in your facts, and in a public hearing it can sound like church members would get priority for the units. That raises fair housing questions you don't want. I'd cut it.

4. **"These are the people on that waiting list."** This is an overclaim. The housing authority's list may be for vouchers or public housing with different income limits, and the people on it won't automatically get these units. "Households like the ones on that list" is accurate and just as strong.

5. **"My neighbors and I can handle a slower light."** Some of those neighbors may be in the room speaking against the project. Speak only for yourself: "I can handle a slower light." Along the same lines, "the choice isn't close" can sound dismissive of people with real concerns. Something like "I've weighed it, and I come down clearly on the side of approval" makes the same point without the edge.

## Weaker than it could be

6. **The traffic rebuttal relies on your opinion.** "A D rating doesn't mean gridlock" is true, but commissioners respond better to their own standards. Check two things in the traffic study and county plan:
   - Does the county's comprehensive plan or traffic policy treat a D rating at rush hour as acceptable? Many do. If so, "the study shows the intersection still meets the county's own standard" is your best line on traffic.
   - Does the study give the actual delay in seconds, or the number of rush-hour trips? 310 trips a day is spread across the whole day, and the rush-hour number is probably much smaller. A concrete figure beats "slightly longer wait."

7. **The job list (cashiers, home health aides, teachers' aides, seniors) is made up.** It's not in your facts. It's fine as illustration, but it sounds like data. Either frame it as "people like…" or cut it to save time.

8. **There's no link to what the commission actually decides.** Commissioners vote on whether a project meets zoning and the comprehensive plan, not whether it's a good idea. If you know what the application is asking for (a rezoning, a conditional use permit, a variance), one sentence connecting the county's 2,100-home shortage to its own housing goals would help. Then ask for that specific action, not just "approve this project."

## Length

The draft is about 450 words, which is right at 3 minutes for a calm speaker. Nervous people at a podium speak slower and pause more, and if you get cut off, you lose the ending, which is your best part. Aim for about 380 words. The easiest cuts are the "watched this neighborhood change over 19 years" paragraph (it's generic, and your 19 years is already in the opening), the job list, and "We rarely find land like this."

## Priorities

If you only fix a few things, do these: disclose your church membership, have an answer on Sunday parking, cut the church-members line, and say "I" instead of "my neighbors and I." The rest makes the speech stronger, but those four protect your credibility.

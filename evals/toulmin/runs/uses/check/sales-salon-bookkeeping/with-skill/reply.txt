Mostly yes. It's specific, plain and honest, and a salon owner will believe it. The writer used nearly all your facts and didn't puff any of them up. The weak spots are a few places where a fact is stated but the objection it raises is left hanging, and a few details you never gave the writer.

## Where it's weak, most important first

**1. Sundays and Mondays are probably your reader's free days.** Many salons close on Sunday and Monday, so that's when owners catch up on paperwork. The draft states your hours, which is right, but doesn't answer the obvious reply: "So when I finally sit down with my numbers, I can't reach you?" It needs your answer. Can they email you then? How fast do you reply? Do they book a time? Only you know, so the writer couldn't have added it, but the gap is there.

**2. The missed-deadline paragraph admits the problem but doesn't answer it.** "I don't hide it, and I work hard to make sure it doesn't happen" asks the reader to let it go. Being upfront about it is right. The answer is what's missing, and it's a fact only you have:
- How late were those three months?
- Was it three months for everyone, or three client-months out of roughly 370?
- What did you change afterwards?
- Do clients hear from you before a deadline slips?

If it was 3 out of about 370 closes, saying so turns a weakness into a strong record. Also cut "I don't hide it." It praises you for being honest in the same breath as being honest.

**3. "Someone who stays" doesn't match the fact under it.** The fact is that your clients stay with you. The heading says you stay, which is a different promise. Your retention is one of your best points: two clients left, and both had sold their salons. It deserves a heading that says what it actually shows.

**4. The price paragraph claims more than your facts show.**
- "No surprise increases" sits awkwardly next to telling the reader you raised prices by a third in January. A careful reader will think "so it could go up again."
- "No introductory deals" came from the writer, not from you.

The useful point hiding here is retention through the price rise. Check the dates. "Last year" means 2025. If the raise was January 2026, then the "only two left" figure comes from before the raise. If nobody has left since the raise, that's the strongest thing you can say about price, and the draft doesn't say it. If it's true, add it. If it isn't, cut "no surprise increases."

**5. The strongest answer to "$199 is a lot" is buried.** Price is your reader's main objection. Your best answer to it is accountants charging less because the books arrived clean (9 clients, one saving $800), and it's sitting in the middle of the page. Move it closer to the price. Keep the hedge: 9 out of 31 is under a third, so it shouldn't read as a typical result.

**6. Check every detail you didn't supply.** The writer added things you may or may not do:
- booth rent
- commission splits
- tips running through the card machine
- payroll "tips included"
- "a clear report showing where your money went"
- "When you call, you get me," which implies you pick up the phone

Each one is a promise. Booth rent is the one to check most carefully: booth renters usually aren't on payroll, so does your package cover them? Keep only what's true.

**7. Smaller points:**
- **Staff limit:** the page never says what happens to a salon with more than 10 staff. Even one line helps.
- **Length:** the draft runs a bit over your 350 words. Tightening points 2 and 4 would fix that.
- **The call:** the button tells people the call is free and takes 20 minutes, but not what they'll get from it. A line on that would make more people click.

## What I'd change, in short
Answer the Sunday/Monday question and the missed deadlines with your own facts. Fix the "stays" heading. Check the dates on the price rise and either add retention since the raise or cut "no surprise increases." Move the accountant savings up next to the price. Remove any detail you can't stand behind. The tone and structure are fine as they are.

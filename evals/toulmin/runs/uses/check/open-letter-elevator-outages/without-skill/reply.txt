# Will it work on management?

Partly. It's calm, factual and well organized, and a property manager will take it seriously. It doesn't say anything they can easily wave off as exaggerated, and it answers the inspection certificate before they can use it. But it gives them little reason to act quickly. Here's where I'd push.

## Biggest weaknesses

**1. The tone fits a private letter, but this one is going to TV.**
"We don't want a fight" will sound hollow to someone who first sees the letter on the evening news. Decide which message you're sending:
- **Option A:** Send it privately first with a deadline, and say what happens if they miss it ("If we have not heard from you by X, we will share this letter publicly").
- **Option B:** Go public now and drop the "no fight" line.

Option A usually gets more done. The threat of publicity is often more useful than the publicity itself.

**2. It has no consequences.**
Nothing happens if they ignore it. You could name a next step: complaints to the city housing or buildings department, a request for a re-inspection, or asking residents with disabilities to file accommodation requests. Have someone check your local tenant law before you mention any legal right, but naming *some* escalation path gives the letter weight.

**3. The strongest point is in the middle.**
The repeated "6 to 8 weeks, same as June" is the most damning fact you have, and it sits in paragraph three. Move it to the top. Also add the June question ("what happened to the first order?") to the numbered list. Right now it's only in the body, so a reply can skip it.

**4. The second-elevator request may let them dismiss everything.**
At $400,000, with a claim that the building can't support it, management can reply "not feasible" and feel they've answered the letter. Asking for the feasibility study is smart. But the list is missing the middle-ground requests they could actually say yes to:
- a faster or alternative source for the part
- a service contract that guarantees response times
- a log of every outage
- modernizing the existing elevator

Give them something easy to agree to.

**5. The human impact is generic.**
"Getting to a doctor's appointment… can become hard or impossible" is a guess, not a fact. One real example would do far more, with the resident's permission: a missed dialysis appointment, someone stuck for the 12 days in August. TV especially needs a face.

**6. The emergency line can be rebutted.**
Elevators aren't used in a fire anyway, so management could dismiss this. A medical emergency is the stronger case, such as paramedics carrying someone down nine floors. Be specific, or cut it.

## Smaller fixes

- **Advance notice:** Breakdowns can't be announced ahead of time, so "advance notice whenever possible" only covers planned maintenance. Say that, and ask for prompt notice when the elevator breaks.
- **"A contact person who answers"** and **"what you should want too"** read as small jabs. Fine for the lobby, but they slightly undercut the reasonable tone with management.
- **Who decides:** A $400k decision probably belongs to the building owner, not the manager. Consider cc'ing the owner.
- **Deadline:** Fill in a specific one (10 to 14 days). "[date]" is easy to forget at the last minute.
- **Check the facts:** This is going public, so confirm the numbers. Is it 41 full days or partial days? Do 74 households equal 74 apartments? And date the second "6 to 8 weeks" statement.
- **The TV station** will need a short separate cover note with a contact name and phone number, not just the letter.
- **Lobby readers** aren't told what to do. For the posted version, add a line pointing them to the association.

## Bottom line

Fix points 1 through 3 and the letter goes from polite to hard to ignore.

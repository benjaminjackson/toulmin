**It's mostly there.** The letter is calm and specific, and the asks are concrete enough that management can't easily wave them away. A property manager reading it will probably pass it to the owner or a lawyer, and nothing in it is easy to dismiss. Its weak spots are mostly about pressure (what happens if they ignore it) rather than tone.

## Where it's weak

**1. It doesn't say what happens if management says no.**
The letter asks for a written reply by a date, then stops. Management's easiest response is a polite note saying "the part is on order," which is what they've already said twice. If the association will take a next step (for example, contacting the city housing or buildings department, or a tenant rights group), say so plainly, once, near the deadline. Only include a step you'll actually take. If there isn't one, the letter's real leverage is the TV station, which leads to the next point.

**2. It's written for two readers and only admits to one.**
"We don't want a fight" reads oddly in a letter management will first see on the local news. They'll read the whole thing as an attack, whatever the tone. You have two honest options:
- Send it privately first with a deadline, and go public only if they miss it.
- Go public at once, and say so in the letter ("We are sharing this letter with residents and with [station]").

Either works. What weakens you is a friendly letter whose existence is the unfriendly part.

**3. The strongest fact is in the letter but not connected to anything.**
A part ordered in June with a 6–8 week lead time was due between late July and mid-August. The 12-day outage happened in August. Put those side by side and the "same thing again" line goes from a complaint to evidence. Right now the August outage sits in the first paragraph and the June promise in the third. Also give the date of the second promise. "Now" is vague.

**4. The emergency sentence invites an easy comeback.**
Elevators aren't used for fire evacuation anyway, so management can answer "the elevator has nothing to do with evacuation" and look reasonable. The real risk is medical emergencies: paramedics carrying someone down as many as eight flights. Either make it specific in that way or cut it. As written it's vague, and the vague version is the one they can rebut.

**5. Two lines suggest facts you didn't give the writer.**
- "A contact person who answers" implies nobody currently answers.
- "We have not seen the reasoning behind it" may be true, but it wasn't in your facts.

In a letter going to a TV station, every factual implication should be something you can back up. Keep these lines if they're true, cut them if they're not.

**6. The second-elevator section could hurt you with the public.**
74 households is strong, and asking for the assessment rather than demanding the elevator is the right move. But viewers will hear "$400,000" and might side with the landlord. That makes this the easiest part of the letter to attack, and it pulls attention from the demands you can actually win: the repair timeline and the mobility plan. Asking management to pay for a new study they've already said is pointless is also likely to be refused. Consider making this request #4 and the meeting #3, so the order matches what's most winnable.

**7. The human cost is all numbers.**
"23 over 75, 6 use wheelchairs or walkers" is good for management. For the TV station and the lobby, one concrete case would carry more, such as a resident who missed a medical appointment in August. Only include one if it's real and the person agrees to it.

## Smaller things

- One more figure your facts support: 41 days out of roughly the 320 so far this year is about **one day in eight**. That's easier to picture than "41 days." Check it against the letter's actual date.
- "41 days of outages speak for themselves" is a cliché, and the paragraph has already made that point. You could cut it.
- Set the response deadline so it fits with the 30-day meeting request. For example, a written reply in 14 days and a meeting within 30.
- The draft runs closer to 470 words than 400. Cutting the emergency sentence and the cliché gets you most of the way.
- For the TV station, send a short cover note with a contact name and phone number. Reporters need someone to call.

**The changes that matter most:** link the June promise to the August outage, decide openly whether this is private-then-public or public now, and add a real consequence for missing the deadline.

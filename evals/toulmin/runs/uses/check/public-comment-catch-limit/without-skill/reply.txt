# Will the draft work on the commission?

Mostly yes. It's specific and calm, and it's built on your real numbers, which is what moves a commission. Staff and commissioners hear a lot of "this will ruin us." A captain who shows his 2016 bookings stands out.

The writer also left out your line that every charter boat in the harbor will go out of business. Don't put it back. Your own 2016 numbers argue against it: you lost about 19% of trips and recovered to about 91% within two years, and you stayed in business. A commissioner who catches that will discount everything else you say. The draft's version, a real and measurable loss that lasts years, is the stronger claim because you can prove it.

# Where it's weak

**1. Your request doesn't match your argument.** That's the biggest problem. The 9% vs. 80% point says charter boats are a small share of the catch, so cutting their limit gains little. But then you ask for a 3-fish limit for everyone, which mostly affects the private boats, not you. Commissioners will notice the mismatch. If the 9% argument is your core logic, the matching request is a separate limit for charter boats, or at least asking staff to analyze one. Otherwise, drop or soften the 9% point and defend the 3-fish request on its own.

**2. "A small gain to the stock" has no number behind it.** Staff will know how much the 2-fish limit reduces harvest compared with 3 fish. If you don't know, the commission can answer you with one sentence. Either get that figure from the staff report or turn it into a question: "What does 2 fish achieve that 3 fish doesn't?"

**3. It skips the 78% figure.** "I'm not here to argue with the science" is good, but the stock is below target and the commission has to act. Saying the 78% out loud shows you've read the report. It also lets you frame your request as still rebuilding the stock, just at a cost the harbor can survive. Without it, your alternative sounds like "do less" with no conservation case.

**4. Two lines claim more than your facts show.**
- "I never got all the way back": you have data through two years after the cut (155 trips). "Never" covers every year since. Say what you know, or check your later numbers.
- "This proposal would last three years, not one": this suggests the 2016 cut lasted only one year. If it lasted longer, someone will correct you on the record.

**5. "Our harbor" rests on one boat's data.** The draft names 11 boats but only shows your numbers. If you can get even a few other captains' 2016 booking drops before the hearing, "heavy cost on our harbor" becomes evidence instead of a guess. That's the most valuable thing you could add.

**6. The survey will be seen as self-interested.** It was run by the charter association, and it measures what customers say they'll do, not what they actually do. It's fine to use, but it backs up your 2016 numbers rather than standing on its own. Also, 41 + 12 is 53, so 17 people are unaccounted for. Know what they said in case you're asked.

**7. The "two fish" line works but slightly undercuts the survey.** "People don't drive to the coast… to take home two fish" is memorable. Just know that your own survey has 12 people saying they'd come anyway.

# Delivery

- **Length:** it's about 280 words, which is right at the edge of two minutes. Read it aloud with a timer. If you run long, the closing request is what gets cut off, and that's the part that matters most.
- **Put the request first.** Say it in one sentence near the top, then again at the end. Commissioners sit through hours of comments and remember the people who told them what they wanted.
- **Too many numbers in a row.** Paragraphs 2–4 stack figures that are hard to follow by ear. When you practice, decide which two or three numbers you want them to remember (likely the 32 lost trips / $29,000 and the 9% vs. 80%) and stress those.

Also submit a written comment with the full data: survey details, other captains' numbers, and your booking history. Keep the spoken version short and point to the written one.

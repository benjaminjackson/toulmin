**Mostly yes.** It's plain and specific, and it sounds like a farm manager wrote it, which is what an admissions committee trusts. The 3 a.m. shift and the mastitis catches are exactly the kind of concrete evidence that makes a reference believable. But there are a few real problems, and the first two matter most.

## 1. It states things you didn't tell the writer
You're signing this, so every sentence has to be true. Check these:
- **"Learned it on his own initiative"** and **"When we brought in our herd management software"**: you only said he learned it. Was it his idea? Was the software new?
- **"Came back to the same shift and the same standard of work"**: you never said this. If it's true, keep it, because it's the most important line in that paragraph. If it isn't, it has to go.
- **"Seriously ill"**: you said "got sick."
- **"He did not need anyone to tell him to do that"**: did he take the night class on his own?
- **"A real feel for animals"** and **"growing interest in the technology"**: these are reasonable, but they're the writer's guesses, not your facts.

## 2. Attendance contradicts itself
"In three years he has missed two shifts" is followed later by a five-week absence. A careful reader will notice, and it makes the first claim look like spin. Add the qualifier where the claim is made, e.g. "apart from a family emergency."

## 3. The absence paragraph could hurt him
The committee would never know about the five weeks unless you told them. Once they do, "I would expect the same of him in your program" can read as a warning that he might leave mid-semester. You have two honest options:
- **Leave it out.** It isn't relevant to whether he'll succeed in the program, and leaving it out isn't dishonest.
- **Keep it short and factual.** Say it was a family emergency, that he came back (if true), and say nothing about the future.

The draft also leaves out the 2 days' notice. That's fine, because it's your call and it doesn't help anyone. Just make sure you're comfortable with the paragraph as written, since it's being framed as a strength.

## 4. Thin on what the committee most needs to know
Their real question is whether he can handle coursework. Your best evidence is that he learned the software and turned it into something useful. Specifics would help:
- How quickly did he learn it?
- Does he train or help others on it?
- What would those nine cases have cost if they'd been caught late? Even a rough figure in treatment costs or discarded milk would do.

## 5. Things you know that the letter leaves out
- Compared with others you've hired, how does he rank? "One of the most dependable" is good. "The best of the roughly 30 people I've hired" is stronger.
- Will you keep his job open, offer flexible hours, or hire him back after? If so, say it. It tells the committee you're backing him, not just being polite.
- Does he do anything beyond milking, like training new workers or covering for others?

## 6. Minor points
- The closing line ("a credit to your program") is boilerplate and could go in any letter. Ending on something specific to Tomás would be stronger.
- The English paragraph is handled well. Being upfront about it makes the rest of the letter more believable. Keep it.

**Bottom line:** check the invented details, fix the attendance contradiction, and decide on purpose whether to mention the absence. If you can, add one or two facts about how fast he learns and whether you'll support him through the program.

**Mostly, yes.** The reader is an admissions committee for a two-year technical program. They want to know whether he'll keep up with the coursework and finish. The draft's main argument is "he's been completely reliable for me, so he'll be a reliable student." That's a fair argument for this program, and the 3 a.m. shift backs it up well. The plain voice fits a farm manager. There are four problems, the first two serious.

**1. It adds facts you didn't give.** You'd be signing these, so check every one:
- "Learned it on his own initiative" and "when we brought in our herd management software." You said he learned it. You didn't say how, or when the farm got it.
- "Nobody else is in the barn at that hour."
- "Seriously ill." You said his father got sick.
- "He came back to the same shift and the same standard of work."
- "He did not need anyone to tell him to do that," about the night class.
- "A real feel for animals" and "a growing interest in the technology."

Some of these may be true. Keep the ones you can stand behind and cut the rest. The line about coming back to the same standard matters most, because the whole summer paragraph depends on it.

**2. The summer paragraph works against you.** It says leaving for five weeks shows "he comes back and finishes the job." An admissions reader is more likely to take it the other way: this student might leave for five weeks in the middle of a semester. It also leaves out that he gave you two days' notice, which would make that worry worse if anyone found out. They have no other way to learn about the absence. You have two honest choices:
- **Cut the paragraph.** Then change "missed two shifts" to "missed two scheduled shifts apart from a family emergency." Otherwise the number looks too clean if the absence ever comes up.
- **Keep it but shorten it to one plain sentence.** Say it was a family emergency and he came back. Drop the claim about what it shows.

I'd cut it. As it stands, the paragraph raises a doubt the reader didn't have and then answers it with something you never told the writer.

**3. The English paragraph shows effort, not ability.** Being honest about it is right, and the committee will notice his English anyway. But "he's taking a class" doesn't tell them whether he can handle written coursework now. If you have something concrete, use it, for example:
- He enters records in the software.
- He writes up cases for the vet.
- He reads labels or protocols.
- How far along he is in the class.

That would answer their question much better than effort alone.

**4. The mastitis example is the strongest point and it's too vague.** The letter explains what mastitis is to agriculture faculty, which they already know. What it doesn't say is what Tomás actually noticed. Was it somatic cell counts, drops in milk yield, how the cows behaved? What did the vet say about the cases he flagged? Two specific sentences there would show the technical skill the program trains for, which "pays close attention" doesn't.

**Missing, if it's true:** what you'll do while he's in the program. Will you keep his job, change his hours, or hire him back afterward? A committee worried about whether he'll finish would find that more convincing than anything else in the letter. Only include what you can actually commit to.

One small point: "one of the most dependable people I have hired" is fine as your own judgment, as long as you mean it.

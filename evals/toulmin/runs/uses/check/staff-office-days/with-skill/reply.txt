**Short answer:** It will work on most of the staff, but not on the people most likely to push back. The reasons are honest and specific, and the paragraph about the partners coming in more is the strongest in the email. It's weakest with the 15 people hired as fully remote, and above all with the 11 of them who live within two hours. The email tells them it understands the change and then gives them nothing.

## The main weaknesses, in order of importance

**1. The paragraph to the 15 remote hires is sympathy with nothing behind it.** "This is a real change to what you signed up for. We aren't taking that lightly" names their objection and then asks them to get past it. The layout makes this worse. The bullets suggest three groups with three different deals, but "Most of you" and "Colleagues hired as fully remote" get exactly the same terms. The second bullet just shows that nothing is on offer. Those 11 people will read that bullet more closely than anything else in the email. The partners need to decide one of two things before Friday:
- whether there is anything for this group, such as a transition period, help with travel costs, or a conversation with each of them like the four are getting; or
- that there isn't, in which case the email should say so plainly and give the reason.

Either is better than what's there now. The writer can't fill this in for you.

**2. Check the dates.** The draft says "September 1, 2027" and lets the four stay remote "until the end of 2027." If the email goes out now (November 2026), September 2027 is ten months away, and the four's extension only runs four months past the date everyone else starts coming in. That will look like a token gesture. If you meant something longer, fix the year. If four months is what you meant, be aware it's thin and expect to be asked about it. Either way, the gap before September is a real point in your favour, and the email could say outright that people have about ten months to arrange things.

**3. The hospital-interview argument leaves a cheaper fix open.** A sceptical reader will ask whether the people in those interviews were the remote staff at all. Could the problem be fixed by picking interview teams who already work together, or by rehearsing more? As written, the client feedback supports "we need to look like a team" as well as it supports "everyone in three days a week." You could close the gap with one sentence on why rehearsal isn't enough: a team that knows each other shows it in how they answer unscripted questions. You could also note that the partners' own one day a week was part of the problem, which connects this paragraph to the partners' paragraph.

**4. The 30% figure needs a quick check before it goes out.** "We think much of that comes from…" is the right level of caution. But staff will ask who those slower juniors are. If they're mostly the remote hires, the argument holds. If many of them were in the office already, or if the post-2022 group also had other disadvantages (a thinner project pipeline, less senior attention), someone at the meeting will say so. Know the answer before Friday, even if it stays out of the email.

## Smaller gaps readers will notice

- **Mondays and Fridays aren't mentioned.** If people can work from home on those days, say so. It's the main thing they keep, and right now they have to work it out for themselves.
- **Why three days, and why the same three for everyone?** The reason (everyone overlaps, so the learning-by-proximity actually happens) is implied but never stated. One clause would do it.
- **The email never says what happens to someone who can't do this.** You don't need to answer that in the email, but expect the question on Friday. For people hired on fully remote terms, check their contracts and any consultation requirements before sending. The email reads as a final decision, and that may matter for this group.
- **Practical questions** such as desk space for 46 people on the same three days will come up on Friday. Have answers ready rather than adding them to the email.
- **Small edit:** cut the "Most of you" bullet. It repeats the first line of the email.

## What's working

- Putting the decision first is right.
- Sending it before the meeting, and saying that's why, is a respectful move.
- The junior-development paragraph is framed around what staff lose, not what the firm loses. That's the right angle for this audience.
- The partners' paragraph ("We can't ask you to be here for each other if we aren't") is the most persuasive line in the email.
- The length is right for one page.

The one fix that matters most is item 1. The rest is tightening.

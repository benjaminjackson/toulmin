## Will it work?

Probably well enough to get read seriously, but not yet well enough to win the interview over other candidates. It's honest, plain and well paced. It handles the seven-year gap with dignity and makes clear the caregiving is over. The collections figure is the strongest line in it.

The trouble is that the dentist reading it will have two main questions, and the letter answers neither: **can she run my staff, and can she get my insurance claims paid?**

## Where it's weak

**1. It never says she managed people.** The posting says the manager supervises 9 people, and that may be the centre of the job. The draft says she "ran the front office," which sounds smaller than what she did: she was office manager of a clinic with 18 people. Nothing says how many reported to her, or whether she hired, trained, scheduled or handled problems with staff. This is the biggest gap. She should say how many people she supervised and what that involved. If she supervised more than 9, that's a strong point.

**2. It leaves the vet-to-dental gap for the reader to notice.** The reader will see it anyway. Vet clinics are mostly paid directly by clients. Dental offices live on insurance claims: pre-authorizations, denials, resubmissions. Her collections record is about chasing unpaid client invoices, which isn't the same skill. The paragraph about her mother's claims could bridge that gap, but right now it's aimed the wrong way. "A close view... from the patient's side" helping with "coverage questions" is a soft benefit. The dentist cares about claims getting paid. If she appealed denials, chased insurers or untangled billing errors for six specialists, she should say so in one concrete sentence. Then the gap becomes a strength.

**3. The Dentrix answer is only a promise.** "I'm ready to start learning Dentrix right away" is what every candidate says. Her last software change was in 2014, so the reader may also wonder whether she's current. If she does something before sending the letter, she can name it: start Dentrix's online training, take a short course, or try a demo. One true sentence like "I've started Dentrix's online training" does more than any promise. Also, Dentrix sits in its own paragraph near the end, which makes it look bigger than it is. It belongs right after the AVImark sentence, where her record of learning one system answers the question about the next.

**4. Two claims are asserted without proof.** "I know what it takes to change systems without disrupting patients or staff" is just a statement. One detail would back it up: how long the switch took, whether she trained the staff, whether the clinic kept seeing patients throughout. The collections figure would also land harder with a few words on how she did it.

**5. The last paragraph is filler.** "Organized and steady, and I care about making an office run well" could come from any applicant, and it costs about 40 words she needs for points 1 and 2. Cut it or shrink it to one line.

**6. "In my own town" is underused.** For a 52-year-old applicant, being local and done with caregiving is a real answer to the worry the reader may have but won't say: will she stay? One clause saying she plans to stay could make that point.

## What I'd change, in priority order

1. Add supervision: how many people, what she did as their manager.
2. Point the insurance paragraph at getting claims paid, not at patient conversations.
3. Do something real on Dentrix before sending, state it, and move it next to AVImark.
4. Add one concrete detail each to the software switch and the collections drop.
5. Cut the generic closing paragraph to make room. The draft is close to the 300-word limit, so the space has to come from there.
6. Change "ran the front office" to "was office manager."

Points 1, 2 and 3 need facts only she has, so ask her for them before revising. Don't fill them in with guesses.

**Short answer:** The draft is honest and well organised, and it handles the late report better than most applicants would. It won't work yet, for two reasons. The outcome it promises is small for $50,000, and it leaves open the two questions a program officer will ask first: can you afford to run the van, and what will it do outside harvest season?

## 1. The writer didn't write "double," and was right not to

Your numbers don't support doubling:

- You hand out about 6,000 lbs of fresh food a month, which is about 72,000 lbs a year.
- Doubling means about 72,000 extra lbs in the first year.
- Last summer's farm offers were 9,000 lbs. That's about a 12.5% rise over the year, or 50% during the three harvest months only.

So doubling would take roughly eight times the farm food you were offered. The writer chose the accurate "50 percent during the harvest months" and left a bracket for "other fresh food sources." But they didn't tell you they'd dropped your main claim, and you should know that.

What to do:
- **If you have other sources the van opens up**, like grocery store pickups, a dairy, a regional food bank's cold stock, or farms that would give more once they know you can collect, list each with estimated pounds. Then see what total you actually reach. That might back "double," or something like "increase by half."
- **If you don't have those sources**, don't write "double." Foundation staff can do this arithmetic, and a goal you miss in your final report will hurt you, especially since that report is the next one they'll read.

## 2. The outcome is small for the money

As written, the measurable result is 9,000 lbs of produce in year one. That's about $5.50 of grant money per pound, from a van that sits idle nine months of the year. A reviewer will think of the cheaper option: rent a refrigerated truck for the summer, or ask the farms to deliver. The draft doesn't say why you need to own one.

The answer is year-round use. You need to say what the van does from October to June, in pounds. This is the same gap as point 1, and it's the most important thing to fill.

## 3. Storage is a hole

You told the writer the problem was collecting food cold *and* storing it cold. A van fixes collection. The draft says the van keeps food cold "from pickup to distribution," which only holds if you hand food out on the day you collect it, or if your current fridges have room for about 3,000 extra lbs a month. A reviewer will ask where the food goes when it arrives. Answer that in one sentence.

## 4. Running costs and reserves look risky together

The draft makes spending more than 60% of your reserves sound like commitment. A funder will also see a volunteer-run group dropping to $11,000 in reserves while taking on insurance, fuel and maintenance, with the cost left as "$[X]" and the funding source left as "[source]." That blank is the second thing a reviewer looks for. You need:
- a real yearly running cost
- where that money will come from
- ideally, a sense of how many months of operating costs $11,000 covers

If you can't fill this, the reserves line hurts you more than it helps.

## 5. Two blanks that only you can fill

- **What the 2023 shelving grant achieved.** This is your best evidence that you deliver on grants. Give a number if you have one.
- **What you changed after the late report.** The writer suggested examples. Only write in something you have actually done. If nothing has changed yet, make a real change before you submit, then describe it. The admission plus a concrete fix is strong. The admission plus a vague promise is weak.

## 6. Smaller points

- **"Grown a few miles from families who needed it."** You never gave a distance. Check it or cut it.
- **"There's plenty of fresh food nearby."** This rests on one summer of offers. It will read as stronger if a farm is willing to say so, for example in a support letter saying it would donate again.
- **Drivers.** Four drivers for weekly runs is solid. A reviewer might ask whether your insurer has requirements for drivers, so have an answer ready even if it doesn't go in the narrative.
- **Order.** If the foundation's form doesn't set the section order, consider putting the shelving grant and the late report earlier. Then the reviewer reads how you fixed the late report before your request, not after it.
- **Length.** The draft is around 450 words with blanks still in it, and filling them will push past 500. Cut the conclusion first, since it only repeats the opening. The "Need" section could also lose a sentence.

## Priority order

1. Year-round sources and pounds, then decide whether "double" survives.
2. Running costs and how you'll pay for them.
3. Cold storage capacity.
4. What the shelving grant achieved, and the real fix for late reporting.

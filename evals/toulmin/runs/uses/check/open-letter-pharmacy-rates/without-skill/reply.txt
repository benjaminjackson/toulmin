**It's honest and well organized, but it probably won't move the plan.** The board will read it as a reasonable complaint they can answer with a polite reply. Editors may skip it because the news (the July 1 deadline) is buried. Here's where it's weak.

**1. The "third will close" claim is missing, and the writer didn't tell you.** I think leaving it out was right, but you should have been told why. None of your facts support it. You don't know why the nine pharmacies closed, so you can't project that a third will close in a year. The first thing a reporter or the plan's spokesperson will ask is "where does a third come from?" If the answer is "it's our sense of things," the story becomes "pharmacists couldn't back up their claim," and that undercuts the $1.80 figure too. If you want a forward-looking warning, get a real number. Survey the 38 this week: "If rates don't change by July 1, will you leave the plan's network, cut hours, or close?" "17 of the 38 pharmacies signing this letter say…" is a fact you can defend, and it's stronger than a guess about the whole state.

**2. The letter leaves out why you can't just leave the network.** The plan will think: if you lose money, stop taking our members. Your answer is the 22%. Losing almost a quarter of your prescriptions would also close you. So you're stuck filling at a loss or losing a large share of your business. Right now the letter only calls 22% "a large share of our business." Say outright that you're stuck.

**3. The 3,100 figure doesn't lead anywhere.** It's stated and then dropped. Readers will want to multiply it by $1.80, but you can't. The $1.80 applies only to the top 20 generics, not every prescription. Either find out how many of your 3,100 were those drugs and give the real yearly loss, or cut the number.

**4. The $1.80 will be challenged and isn't defended.** Expect the PBM to say it's an average over a hand-picked set of drugs, and that they pay more on brand-name drugs. One sentence on how you calculated it (invoice cost plus what it costs to dispense) and an offer to share the data with the plan would cover that.

**5. Ask #1 is too vague.** "Covers what it actually costs" lets the plan agree in principle and do nothing. Name a benchmark, such as a published acquisition-cost benchmark like NADAC plus a set dispensing fee, which is the approach many state Medicaid programs use. Concrete asks are harder to brush off.

**6. The deadline is in paragraph five.** For the newspapers, the July 1 renewal is the news. Put it in the first paragraph. Also give a date for the meeting request, since the contract will be negotiated well before July 1, and include a contact person.

**7. The 14 counties are abstract.** Name them, or at least say how many plan members live there. Legislators and local reporters respond to their own county's name.

**8. Newspaper readers don't know what a PBM is.** Add one sentence: the plan hires a company that sets what pharmacies get paid, and that contract is up for renewal.

**Smaller issues:**
- The letter switches from "we" to "I" without saying who "I" is. Introduce yourself before your own pharmacy's numbers.
- The closures paragraph refers to "those closures," but the previous paragraph was about past closures, so it reads unclearly.
- A list of 38 pharmacies and towns won't fit on the same page as the letter. Plan on a second page or an attachment.

**What I'd keep:** the opening line about teachers, troopers and nurses, the honest admission about the nine closures (it makes everything else more believable), and the "drive farther, wait for mail order, or go without" sentence.

**Short answer:** Mostly yes. The tone fits the reader: an adult with type 2 diabetes who has probably been sold miracle fixes before and has learned to be skeptical. The draft sounds like a real dietitian talking, not a supplement ad. Where it's weak is that it's vague about the things a buyer checks right before paying.

## The A1C claim: the writer was right not to say it

You asked for "our meals will lower your A1C." The draft doesn't say that, and I'd keep it that way.

- **Your data can't support it.** You have 14 people out of 88, and they chose to share their results. People whose numbers went up are less likely to send them. There's no comparison group, and you don't track medication changes, so a drop could come from a new prescription.
- **It's risky.** A specific promise about a health outcome is the kind of claim the FTC goes after, and it could also cause problems with your dietitian credential. Your license is worth more than one line of copy.
- **It would backfire with this reader.** People with diabetes know A1C depends on medication, activity, stress and illness. A guarantee makes you sound like everyone else.

The honest numbers do the job better. "10 of 14 went down" is believable precisely because the page also says one went up.

**One thing to check:** "average drop of 0.6 points" is ambiguous in your notes and in the draft. Is it the average of the 10 who went down, or of all 14? Confirm which before it's published. If it's only the 10, the sentence needs to say so.

**One thing I'd add:** a short line saying you don't track medication changes. Saying that yourself builds trust and heads off the obvious objection.

## Where it's weak

1. **You aren't on the page.** Your credential is the main reason to buy, but there's no name, photo or one-line story. "Planned by me, a registered dietitian" needs a real person behind it.
2. **No menu.** "Real food you'll want to eat" is a claim with nothing to back it up. Three or four actual dishes with their carb counts would do more than that whole bullet.
3. **No customer voices.** You have 37 people who've stayed more than six months. "People stay because the meals fit their lives" is you speaking for them. Two short quotes from those customers would be much stronger.
4. **Missing logistics.** These questions decide whether someone signs up:
   - Is 10 meals lunch and dinner for five days? What about weekends?
   - How long do meals keep after Sunday delivery? Is Friday's dinner still fresh?
   - Which areas do you deliver to?
   - Can you handle allergies or restrictions (no pork, low sodium, kidney diet)?
   - Does the trial week roll into the weekly plan automatically? How do people cancel? Not answering this costs trust at the moment someone is deciding to pay.
5. **45 grams isn't explained.** The number is good, but most readers won't know why it matters. One sentence would help, along the lines of "fits the per-meal targets many care teams recommend, and it's the same every time, so your numbers are easier to read." Avoid saying it matches one official standard, because there isn't one.
6. **"Less than most takeout"** is a claim you haven't checked. It's probably true but adds little. Comparing yourself to the work of cooking diabetes-friendly meals fits your pitch better than comparing to takeout.
7. **Caregivers aren't mentioned.** Some buyers will be adult children ordering for a parent. One line, or "gift a trial week," could open up that market.

## What the draft got right (keep these)

- It leaves out the 1-in-5 trial conversion rate. That's an internal number and doesn't belong on the page.
- The medical disclaimer is short and doesn't read like legal text.
- The trial-week call to action is low-pressure and suits this reader.
- It's close to your 400-word target. Adding the items above means cutting elsewhere, and the "How it works" intro is the easiest place to trim.

**Priority order if you only fix some:** your name and photo, real menu items, logistics and cancellation terms, then customer quotes.

**Yes, mostly.** The tone suits an editor you've worked with for four years. The ask is a single specific number, the evidence is concrete, and it doesn't threaten. An editor can say yes to this, or pass it up the chain, without losing face. Here are the weak spots, most important first.

**1. The writer left out your main line, and was right to, but should have told you.** You wanted to say every other publisher pays at least $12,000. Your facts don't support that. You have one other publisher at $12,500, and your agent's range starts at $11,000, below $12,000. Editors talk to agents and other publishers, so this one may know the going rates. If they catch one overstatement, they'll start doubting your true numbers too. Your real figures are already strong. Leaving that line out was the right call; it just shouldn't have happened without a word to you.

**2. "My rate elsewhere has gone up" stretches one book into a trend.** It's a single fee from a single publisher. Simply stating the $12,500 is stronger than claiming a pattern the editor can ask you to back up.

**3. The workload sentence doesn't help.** "Each book is still about four months of work" says nothing has changed, which is the publisher's argument, not yours. The point that matters is that the fee hasn't moved in four years while costs have. Either make that point or cut the sentence.

**4. Leaning on "my agent tells me" is weak.** Your agent is on your side, so an editor may discount it. It also raises a question: if you have an agent, why aren't they negotiating? Either present the range as the market rate and leave the agent out, or decide whether the agent should send this. Pick one before you send.

**5. The sales argument depends on how you're paid, and the draft doesn't know.** If $9,000 is a flat fee, the point is strong: the publisher kept all the benefit of those 20,000+ sales. If it's an advance against royalties, the editor can answer that the sales already paid you through royalties. Check your contracts. If it's flat, say so. That's your best argument.

**6. You haven't decided what happens if they offer less.** A common reply is "We can do $10,000." The draft doesn't hint whether $11,500 is firm, and you don't need to say so in the email. But decide your lowest acceptable fee before they answer.

**7. "I'd be glad to do this one" comes before the fee discussion.** It's warm, but it can read as a yes already given. "Before we finalize" partly fixes this. Consider whether you want to sound quite that committed.

**What to keep:** the "what our books have achieved together" framing, the point that $11,500 is still below what you've been paid elsewhere, the offer of a phone call, and the length.

**Mostly yes. It will work on an admissions committee, partly because it doesn't do what you asked.** The draft doesn't say the findings are ready for a top journal. It says that "with further sampling this work has a real chance of becoming a peer-reviewed publication." The writer changed this without telling you, and you should know that. I think the change is right. Your readers will be marine ecologists. They'll see six sites, a positive result at only two of them, one site missing three months of data, no repeat study, and only a poster so far. A "ready for a top journal" claim on those facts would make them doubt everything else in the letter, including your praise of Priya. Committees aren't judging whether the thesis can be published. They're judging her, and the facts you have about her are strong.

**Where it's weak:**

1. **It contains claims you didn't give the writer.** You should check each one or remove it.
   - "Priya *designed* a 14-month study." Did she design it, or did you?
   - "She knows her results come from a single bay..." and "she is eager to test whether the marsh effect holds up." These describe what she thinks. Are they true?
   - "A single bay and a single **season**" is wrong. The study ran 14 months, which is more than a year. A reader who notices will wonder what else is careless.

2. **Some placeholders do real work.** The sentence about how she handled the storm gap is the most important blank in the letter. If she handled it well (for example, she adjusted the analysis or was open about the gap), it's strong evidence of her judgment. If you can't say something specific, cut the storm sentence completely. Raising a problem without saying how she solved it hurts her. The course anecdote matters too. Right now "scientific curiosity" in the closing has nothing behind it. Every other quality in the letter (persistence, field skills) has evidence.

3. **There's no comparison to other students.** You've supervised about 30 theses. That's the most persuasive fact you have, but the letter only says she's "one of the most capable... I have worked with." If it's true, say something like "her thesis is among the two or three best of the roughly 30 I've supervised." Committees weigh specific rankings much more heavily than adjectives.

4. **The publication sentence is too hedged.** "A real chance of becoming" sounds lukewarm. Either drop the prediction or state something definite, like "with additional years of data, this is publishable work." Then move straight on to what she did.

5. **Some skills committees look for are missing.** The letter doesn't mention her data analysis or statistics, her writing, or how independently she worked. If any of these are strengths, one sentence on each would help. It also doesn't connect her to the program she's applying to. If she has a target lab or research question, a line about it makes the letter feel written for that program.

6. **It may run longer than one page.** Once the brackets are filled in, the draft will probably go over. You can shorten the paragraph about limitations and the closing paragraph to make room.

**In short:** keep the writer's honest framing of the results, fix "single season," and confirm or cut the invented claims. Fill in the storm-gap and course-anecdote blanks with real details, and add an explicit ranking against your 30 thesis students.

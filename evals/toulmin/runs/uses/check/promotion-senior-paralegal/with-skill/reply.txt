## Will it work?

It will probably get you a meeting. It's clear and honest, and it's short enough for a partner to read in one go. It's less likely to win the decision, because it argues that you've earned the promotion. A managing partner is asking a different question: is it worth $9,000 a year to the group to make this person a senior paralegal? The draft never answers that directly.

## Where it's weak, most important first

**1. The supervision claim is bigger than the evidence.** "I'm already doing much of that work" rests on one fact: you trained three new hires. Supervising is the main thing that separates the senior role, and training isn't the same as supervising. A partner will notice the gap. If you do more than train (assign or balance work, review other paralegals' filings, act as the person they come to, cover when lawyers are out), say so. If you don't, make the claim smaller: you've shown you can develop paralegals, and you want the responsibility formally.

**2. It doesn't argue that the group needs the role.** You have a strong fact here that the draft treats as a personal credit: the group hired three paralegals in two years. That's a group that has grown and may need someone to organise the paralegal work. The draft also doesn't say whether the group already has a senior paralegal or a vacancy. The request reads very differently in each case, so the letter should show you know which one applies.

**3. It never weighs the cost against what you bring in.** The partner will have the $9,000 in mind whether or not you mention it. You billed 90 hours over target. If you know your billing rate, those 90 hours alone may cover the raise, and one sentence saying so would change how the request reads. Don't guess the rate. Use it only if you have it.

**4. The numbers are bare.** "23 cases, two of which went to trial" tells the partner nothing they don't already know. What did you do on the trials? Did you run exhibits, manage witness logistics, or work in court? One concrete line does more than the count. The same goes for the tracking sheet: two groups adopting it is good, but has the group missed a deadline since 2023? Has it saved time?

**5. The deadline paragraph is right to be there, but it needs a result.** Including it is the right call, since the partner almost certainly knows about it, and you take ownership well. What's missing is evidence the fix worked. Has the second check caught anything since March? Also check that "now built into the tool" is literally true for the other two groups' copies. The partner can verify it.

**6. The opening hedges.** "I believe my work… shows I'm ready" is weaker than the facts allow. The five years is also stated and then never used.

**7. The close has no next step or timing.** It's November, and if the firm sets pay at year-end, that's your reason to ask now. Ask for a specific meeting before that decision is made. Talking about pay at the meeting is fine. Don't add anything about what happens if they say no unless it's true and you mean it.

**8. Your four lawyers aren't used.** The partner will probably ask them about you. If any of them would back you, say so, or line them up before you send this.

**9. Minor: the bold headings** make it read like a memo. That's fine for an email, but plain paragraphs would feel more personal.

## What I'd change

Keep the facts and the honest tone. Shift the argument from "I've earned this" to "the group has grown, it needs someone doing this work, I'm already partly doing it, and the cost is covered." Then either back up the supervision claim with more facts or scale it down, and finish with a dated request for a meeting.

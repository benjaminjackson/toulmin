**Mostly, yes.** It's factual, short (about 300 words), and doesn't oversell. A managing partner will read it in a minute and come away with the right numbers. It has a few weak spots, roughly in order of importance.

### 1. The supervision claim goes too far
"I'm already doing much of that work" is the sentence the partner is most likely to push back on. Training three new hires isn't the same as supervising paralegals. If you also assign work, review other paralegals' filings, or field their questions day to day, say so specifically. If you don't, drop the claim. Let the training stand as evidence you're *ready* to supervise, not proof that you already do. A claim the partner can knock down makes the true ones look weaker.

### 2. The deadline error gets too much space
Including it is a reasonable choice. A near-miss on a court deadline is something the partner probably already knows about, and owning it reads as maturity. But right now it's the longest paragraph and the most vivid moment in the letter, so it's what the partner will remember. Cut it to two sentences that end on the fix. "That safeguard is now built into the tool" repeats the sentence before it. Also check the facts: did the second-person check carry over to the two other groups using the sheet? If it did, say so. That turns the story into something that improved the whole firm. If you're sure the partner doesn't know about the error, think harder about whether to raise it at all.

### 3. The opening is generic
"I believe my work… shows I'm ready" could open anyone's request. Your strongest single fact is that you built a tool other groups chose to adopt. Leading with that, or with the hours and trial work, gets the partner's attention in the first two lines.

### 4. It doesn't say why now or whether there's an opening
The letter doesn't say whether the group has a senior paralegal now, or whether this would be a new role. If someone already holds the job, asking to "supervise the other paralegals" needs different framing. If no one does, the case is stronger: the group has grown by three paralegals and has no one formally in charge of them. That's a reason for the firm to act, not just a reason you deserve it. Add one sentence about it.

### 5. The forward-looking part is vague
"How the group's paralegal work is organized" doesn't commit to anything. Name one concrete thing you'd do as senior paralegal, like a standard onboarding process or a workload-balancing system across the four lawyers. That shows you've thought about the job, not just the title.

### 6. Specifics the partner can't check from the letter
"Two of which went to trial" says nothing about what you did on those trials. If you ran exhibits, managed the trial binders, or handled anything else notable, a short clause helps. Likewise, if you know the tracking sheet saved time or caught other errors, one number would help. Only add what you can back up.

### Small points
- **Pay line:** Mentioning pay is fine, and leaving out the $9,000 figure is right, since the partner knows it. "Including the pay that comes with the position" sounds a little hesitant. Either say it plainly or drop it, since the pay comes with the title anyway.
- **Bold headers:** They make a short email read like a memo. That's fine if your firm's culture is formal, and a bit stiff if you talk to this partner every day.
- **Possible forwarding:** The partner may pass this to firm management or HR. The concrete numbers already make that easy, which is another reason to keep every claim one you can defend.

The fixes that matter most are toning down the supervision claim and shortening the error paragraph. The rest is polish.

# Will this work for the people it's meant for?

Mostly, yes. The draft is clear and honest, and it gets the important facts in early. The 14 affected staff will learn right away that they still have a job and won't lose pay. The writer also avoided several common mistakes: there's no corporate cheerleading, and the hours change is stated plainly instead of being buried. There are some real gaps, though, and one choice you should decide on knowingly.

## 1. The draft leaves out your main message, and I think that's the right call

You asked for the announcement to say this change will make everyone's job better. The draft doesn't say that, and the writer didn't tell you they'd dropped it. I'd keep it out, because your own facts don't back it up:

- **The 14 are giving up things.** They lose 9-to-5 hours for rotating shifts that can run until 8 p.m. They also move from face-to-face work to phone and chat. Many people who chose branch work did so for exactly those reasons.
- **The other five branches see no change.** For them, "better" doesn't mean anything.

If you tell people this is better for them while they're worrying about evening childcare, they'll stop trusting the rest of the email. The draft makes a narrower claim you can defend: 9-minute hold times put "real strain on everyone answering those calls," and the center fixes that. Remote work two days a week and the pay guarantee are real benefits, and the draft states them. If you want more positive framing, build on those specific points rather than adding a blanket claim.

## 2. Sequencing

The draft says "I want to share an important decision with you directly," but it's an email to all staff. If Maple Street and Route 9 staff first learn their branch is closing from this email, at the same moment as everyone else, that will hurt more than anything in the wording. They should hear it in person from you or their manager first. Then this email goes out the same day, and the opening line changes to reflect that.

## 3. Missing facts the 14 will need right away

- **Where the center is.** This is the biggest gap. Their commute is the first thing they'll want to know, and it could decide whether they take the job.
- **How shifts rotate.** "Some of your days will start earlier or end later" is too vague for someone who has to arrange childcare. They need to know how often they'd work late, how much notice they'd get, and whether there's a premium for evening shifts. If those details aren't settled, say when they will be, and don't set the decision deadline before then.
- **Severance details.** Is there a cap or a minimum? How are partial years counted? Do benefits continue? Do they have to sign a release? Someone with one year of service gets two weeks of pay, and they'll want to know whether that's all.
- **Training risk.** What happens if someone has a hard time in the first 90 days? Is the job secure while they're training?
- **"Same pay or higher."** What decides whether someone gets higher pay? If you leave this unexplained, people will start comparing notes and guessing.

## 4. The other five branches

"Your location, role and hours aren't changing" answers today's question but not the one they'll actually be asking: *are we next?* The draft doesn't explain why these two branches were chosen. A sentence on that, and on whether more closures are planned, would help. Even "no further closures are planned" is better than saying nothing. Also, the placeholder about members moving to nearby branches needs real content. Staff at those nearby branches will see their workload go up.

## 5. Smaller points

- The closing line, "build something members will really feel," is vague, and it's the only spot where the draft sounds like a slogan. Something concrete, like cutting the 9-minute wait, would land better.
- "It will suit some of you more than others" is honest and worth keeping.
- There are a lot of placeholders. The length of the decision window is part of the message, so give people enough time.
- The draft runs about 430 words, which is fine. If adding the location, shift details and reasoning makes it longer, cut from the "Why" section, not from the practical details.

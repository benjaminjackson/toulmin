## Your angle

**Don't sell "our meals will lower your A1C." Sell the daily work you take off their plate.** A dietitian plans 20 meals a week with the carbs already controlled, and they arrive cooked. Then offer your A1C results as honest evidence that this tends to help. The ask is the $59 trial week.

## Why not the A1C promise

Your facts support "most customers who shared results saw their A1C go down." They don't support "will lower your A1C."

- **The 14 people chose to share.** That's 14 out of 88, and people whose numbers improved are more likely to send them.
- **There's no comparison group, and you don't know about medication changes.** You can't tell how much of the 0.6-point drop came from the meals and how much from a new prescription.
- **1 person went up and 3 stayed about the same.** "Will lower" is already false for 4 of your 14.

There's also a regulatory risk. A specific promise that a product treats a disease is the kind of claim the FTC and state licensing boards look at closely, and you're making it with your RD credential behind it. Check with your licensing board, or a lawyer who knows FTC health-claim rules, before publishing anything close to a promise.

**A version your facts do support:** "Of 14 customers who shared their A1C before starting and after three months, 10 went down, 3 stayed about the same, and 1 went up. The average change was a drop of 0.6 points. That's a small group and not a clinical study, but it's why I do this." Stating the limits makes the number more believable. People with diabetes have heard plenty of miracle claims.

## The reader

Adults with type 2 diabetes, and possibly a spouse or adult child buying for them. They're likely tired of carb counting, wary of diet promises, and worried about three things: taste, cost, and whether the meals fit their doctor's plan.

## Order for the page

1. **Headline and opening:** the problem of figuring out lunch and dinner every day, and that you solve it.
2. **Who plans it:** you, a registered dietitian, planning every menu yourself. This is your strongest backing, so use it early.
3. **What they get:** 10 cooked meals, each with 45g of carbohydrate or less, delivered Sundays, $129 a week (about $12.90 a meal).
4. **Proof people stay:** 37 of your 88 customers have been with you more than six months, which is 42%.
5. **A1C results:** the honest version above.
6. **Objections:** taste, cost, and "talk to your doctor, especially if you take insulin or other glucose-lowering medication." Fewer carbs can mean lows for those patients, and saying so shows you know your field.
7. **The ask:** try a week for $59.

**Leave out the 1-in-5 trial conversion rate.** It's a business number. It doesn't help the reader decide, and it can read as "80% don't stay."

## What to find out first

1. **The trial week:** is it the same 10 meals? Can people cancel any time after it, or after the full plan starts? Is there a contract?
2. **The A1C numbers:** did you ask all customers, or only some? Is the 0.6 average across all 14, including the one who went up?
3. **Taste:** do you have customer quotes you're allowed to use, or sample dishes you can name?
4. **Other nutrition:** do you also limit sodium or calories? Many readers have high blood pressure too.
5. **Practical details:** the delivery area, the order deadline for Sunday, and whether you handle allergies or other diets.
6. **Who buys:** is it mostly the person with diabetes, family members, or doctor referrals? The answer changes the opening.

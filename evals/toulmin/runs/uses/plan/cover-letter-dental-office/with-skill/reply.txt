The main problem with this letter is that the dentist reading it will have doubts before they get to your first paragraph. Your resume shows a seven-year gap, no dental experience and no Dentrix. So the letter can't just ask for a chance. It has to give an argument that answers each doubt with something you've actually done.

## The angle

**Your claim:** "I've already run a bigger version of this office, and I've already done the hard parts of this job."

**The link you're relying on:** running the front office of a multi-doctor practice is the same job whether the patients are pets or people. That means scheduling several providers, chasing money, supervising staff and moving records onto new software. Leave this link unstated. Show it with the comparison: you ran an office with 4 vets and 14 staff, and theirs has 3 dentists and 9 staff. That's a step down in size for you, not a step up.

**Your strongest fact:** you cut invoices more than 90 days unpaid from about $38,000 to $9,000, which is about a 76% drop in three years. A practice owner cares about that number more than anything else in the letter. Put it in the first paragraph.

## The order (about 300 words)

1. **Opening, about 50 words.** Name the job, then make your claim with the receivables number.
2. **Your record, about 80 words.** You managed an office of 4 vets and 14 staff for 11 years, handling scheduling and billing. Then the 2014 move from paper to software.
3. **Dentrix, about 40 words.** The 2014 migration answers this doubt. You've learned a system and moved an entire clinic onto one, which is harder than learning Dentrix. Add a concrete step you're taking, but only one you'll really take (see question 5).
4. **The gap, about 60 words.** Say it once, plainly: you were your mother's full-time caregiver, she died last spring, and you're ready to work full-time again. Then put those years to work. You handled her insurance claims and her appointments with 6 specialists, so you know human health insurance from the patient's side and what it costs when an office drops a referral.
5. **Close, about 40 words.** Mention that you're local, then ask for a meeting.

## What to leave out

- **Your age.** Don't mention it or hint at it.
- **Your grief and the details of her illness.** One sentence about her is enough. A long account makes the reader worry whether you're ready.
- **Any apology for the gap or for not knowing Dentrix.** State the fact, give the answer and move on.
- **The name AVImark,** unless you need it. "Practice management software" is enough.

## The weak spot to know about

Vet clinics mostly don't bill insurance companies, but dental offices bill them every day. A sharp dentist may notice this. Your mother's claims are your answer, but only if they involved real work. Find out exactly what you did (question 3).

## What to find out first

1. **The reader's name.** Is it the owner dentist or someone else?
2. **Who you supervised.** Did you hire, train or schedule all 14 staff, or some of them? The posting says the manager supervises 9 people, so this matters.
3. **What the insurance work involved.** Did you appeal denied claims, read benefit statements, or coordinate Medicare with a supplemental plan? Did you win anything back?
4. **How you got receivables from $38,000 to $9,000.** One line on the method (new follow-up routine, payment plans, collections policy) makes the number believable.
5. **Anything you can honestly say about Dentrix.** Have you started any training, or will you before an interview? Only say it in the letter if it's true.
6. **Your ties to this practice.** Are you a patient, or do you know anyone there? Also check what else the posting asks for, such as payroll, HIPAA or OSHA, so the letter covers it.

Once you have these answers, the letter can be written to fit under 300 words.

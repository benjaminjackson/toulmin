## The angle

**Make it easy for him to say yes to certainty.** Don't argue about whether $5,650 is fair. Offer him a sure thing: 10 years of tenancy, five more guaranteed, rising rent every year, and no vacancy risk. That's against a higher number that may never get paid.

The idea behind it, which the letter shouldn't spell out: **a landlord does better with a proven tenant at a slightly lower rent than gambling on vacancy.** He already believes this. The unit two doors down has sat empty for 14 months, and he can see it. You only need to mention it once.

## Order

1. **You want to stay and sign five more years.** Lead with this, because it's what he most wants to hear. Mention the 10 years and the kitchen as proof you're committed, not as a debt he owes you.
2. **The late payments, in one sentence, before you ask for anything.** He'll be thinking about them anyway. Thank him for waiving the fees and say you've paid on time every month since (about 2½ years now).
3. **The offer.** $5,100, a 5-year term, 3% increases from year 2. That works out to about $5,253, $5,411, $5,573 and $5,740, so by year 5 he's above his own number.
4. **One line on the market.** The nearby unit has been listed at $28 for 14 months. State it as a fact, not as an accusation.
5. **Close by suggesting you meet to talk it over.** Don't make it an ultimatum.

## Leave out

- **The "18%" and the $29 vs. $28 calculation.** Working through his number for him makes him defensive. The vacant unit makes the point by itself.
- **The coffee chain.** Calling a bluff loses either way. If the interest is real, your best answer is a signed, certain deal. Also note that a café gets little use from a $70k kitchen hood, and probably wants build-out money from him. Keep that in your head, not in the letter.
- **The kitchen as an argument that you're owed something.** It can backfire: a hood ready for the next restaurant makes the space *more* valuable to him.

## Find out first

1. **Your current lease.** Check for a renewal option, a "fair market rent" clause, and notice deadlines. With August about 9 months away, a deadline may be close. This could change everything.
2. **Exact square footage.** If his $5,650 is really $29/sq ft, the space is about 2,340 sq ft. At the neighbor's $28 that's about $5,455, which is still below his ask. Your $5,100 is about $26.
3. **Whether his $5,650 is flat or rises each year.** Over five years, your offer totals about $325k. His totals $339k if flat, and more if it escalates. You need to know the real gap.
4. **Whether the neighbor unit is comparable.** If it's an empty shell with no hood, he'll say yours is worth more. Also check whether it's actually leased by now.
5. **Is $5,100 your opening number or your ceiling?** If it's your ceiling, decide now what else you could trade instead of more rent. Examples are a longer term, a renewal option, or autopay. I've left those for you to choose, not added them to the plan.
6. **Who pays taxes, insurance and shared maintenance costs (CAM)**, and whether that changes in his proposal.

## The angle

**You're already doing the senior paralegal's job. The promotion would make it official.**

The difference between a senior paralegal and a paralegal is supervision, so the case has to rest on supervision. The partner gets there in one step: "people who already do the senior work are the ones to promote." Your strongest facts fit that step directly:

- **You trained the 3 paralegals the group hired in the last 2 years.** That is supervision in all but title, and it's your lead fact.
- **You built the deadline tracking sheet, and two other groups now use it.** That shows you build systems other people rely on, beyond your own 4 lawyers.
- **You added a second-person check after the March error.** That's quality control over other people's work, which is also a supervisor's job.

Your hours (1,640 against a 1,550 target, about 6% over), the 23 cases, the 2 trials and your 5 years show you're a very good paralegal. But those same facts support a different answer just as well: "give them a raise and keep them where they are." Use them to show you have capacity, not as the reason for the promotion. Five years on its own isn't a reason, so mention it once at most.

## Order

1. **The ask, in the first sentence.** Promotion to senior paralegal, and when you'd like it to take effect.
2. **The supervision evidence:** the training, then the sheet and its adoption by two other groups.
3. **The March error, in two plain sentences, next to the fix.** You entered the deadline wrong, a colleague caught it, the filing went in 2 hours before cutoff, and you added a second-person check that now protects all three groups. Leave out excuses and long apologies.
4. **Capacity:** 4 lawyers, 23 cases, 2 trials, 1,640 hours.
5. **What you'd do in the role.** You need to supply this (see below). Without it the piece only looks backwards.
6. **Close:** ask for a meeting.

## Leave out

- **The $9,000.** The partner knows the pay difference. Putting it in makes the letter about money.
- **Comparisons with other paralegals,** and any hint that you'll leave if the answer is no.
- **Case-by-case detail.** The numbers are enough.

**Don't leave out the March error.** The lawyers on that matter know about it, so the partner probably does or will. If you name it with the fix, it shows how you handle a supervisor's responsibility. If you leave it out and they remember it, it looks like you're hiding it.

## Find out first

1. **Is there a senior slot?** Is the role open or budgeted, how many senior paralegals does the group have, and is there a promotion cycle or review date to time this to? If there's no slot, the request changes from "promote me" to "create the role."
2. **Who actually decides?** If the partner needs sign-off from firm management or a committee, write so they can forward it.
3. **Will your 4 lawyers back you?** They're a second audience. Sound out at least one or two before you send it, and name them only if they agree.
4. **Has the second-person check caught anything since March?** One caught error would turn your weakest fact into one of your strongest.
5. **What would you do differently as a senior paralegal?** For example: formal onboarding for new hires, rolling the sheet out to more groups, or reviewing deadlines across the team. One or two concrete commitments.
6. **Would supervising cut your billable hours?** The partner may ask what happens to your 1,640. Have an answer, even if it's only an estimate.

Bring answers to 1, 4 and 5 and the piece mostly writes itself in well under 400 words.

# Strategy for the Tomás reference letter

## The angle

**Tomás is already doing agricultural technology work. The program would give him training for what he's shown he can do.**

Most reference letters for workers say "hardworking and reliable." Yours can say something stronger: he learned your herd management software, uses it to make health calls, and caught 9 mastitis cases early. That's exactly what an ag tech program teaches. The admissions committee's real question is whether he can handle the program and whether he'll finish it. Your evidence answers both: he learns technical tools, and you trust him with the herd on his own at 3 a.m.

## Order

1. **Who you are and how you know him** (2–3 sentences). Farm manager, 400-cow dairy, he's worked for you for 3 years starting at 19. The herd size tells them his job carries real responsibility.
2. **Lead with the software and the mastitis cases.** This is your strongest and most specific point, so put it first. Say what "early" meant in practice: cows saved, milk not dumped, lower treatment costs, or what the vet said.
3. **Trust and responsibility.** He runs the 3 a.m. milking alone, 5 days a week. Say plainly what that means: nobody checks his work, and the herd's health and the farm's income depend on him getting it right.
4. **He keeps learning.** He went from new hire to the person who flags sick cows for the vet. If he agrees, mention that he's taking a night English class on his own. That shows he goes after what he needs, which is a good sign for a student.
5. **Close with a clear, unhedged recommendation.** If it's true, add what you'd do for him: hold his job, offer flexible hours while he studies, or want him back afterward. A future employer backing him is one of the strongest signals a committee can get. Finish with your contact details.

## What to leave out or handle carefully

- **The 2 days' notice.** Leave it out. It was a family medical emergency, and it says nothing about how he'll do as a student.
- **The 5-week absence.** You don't need to bring it up. But **don't write "he's missed only 2 shifts in 3 years"** as a bare number, because that leaves out 5 weeks. If you want to use his attendance, write something honest like "apart from a family medical emergency last summer, after which he returned to his role, he has missed two shifts in three years." Coming back is a point in his favor. Otherwise, drop the number and let the 3 a.m. shift show his reliability.
- **Written English.** Don't call it a weakness. Committees will see his writing in the application anyway. Mention it only through the night class, and only if he's comfortable with that.
- **His father's illness and any personal or immigration details.** Leave them out.
- **Generic praise** ("great attitude," "hard worker") with nothing to back it up. Every claim should rest on something specific.

## Find out first

**From Tomás:**
- Why he wants this program and what he wants to do afterward. One sentence from you connecting his goal to his work makes the letter much stronger.
- Whether he's OK with you mentioning the English class and the family emergency.
- Whether he plans to keep working while he studies.
- Anything he especially wants you to cover, so your letter doesn't repeat his essay.

**From the college:**
- Whether there's a reference form or set questions, and the length, format, deadline, and who to address it to.
- What the program focuses on (herd management, precision ag, equipment), so you can match your examples to it.

**From your records or your vet:**
- Confirm the 9 cases and get the outcome. One line from the vet, or a cost estimate, makes this much more convincing.
- The name of the software, and whether he taught himself or was trained.
- Anything else he's taken on, like training other workers, keeping records, or handling equipment.

**From yourself:**
- What you'll actually offer him (job held, flexible schedule, a role when he finishes). Decide this before you write, because it shapes the closing paragraph.

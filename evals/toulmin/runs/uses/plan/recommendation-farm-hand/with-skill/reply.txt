## The angle

**Tomás already does part of what this program teaches, and he taught himself to do it. Formal training will build on a proven record, and he is very likely to finish.**

The admissions committee is the reader. They are asking two things:
1. **Can he do the work?** The software and the mastitis cases answer this.
2. **Will he finish two years?** Three years of running the 3 a.m. shift alone answers this.

A third question will sit in the background: can he handle the written coursework? The night class answers that one.

The idea connecting your facts to the recommendation is: *someone trusted to run part of a 400-cow operation alone, who picked up the technical tools without being asked, will do well when those tools are taught to him properly.* You don't need to say this outright. If the facts are in the right order, the committee will get there on their own.

## Order on the page

1. **Who you are and your recommendation, in two or three sentences.** Give your role, the herd size, how long you've supervised him, and that you recommend him. Put the recommendation first, not at the end.
2. **The software and the mastitis cases.** This is the most relevant part for an agricultural technology program, so it goes before reliability. Nine early catches is your strongest single fact. It will be stronger still with a comparison or a cost (see below).
3. **The 3 a.m. shift.** He runs it alone, 5 days a week, for a 400-cow herd. Spell out what "alone" means: nobody checks his work and he handles whatever goes wrong. This is your evidence that he'll finish.
4. **Written English, in one or two sentences.** He's stronger spoken than written, he saw that himself and enrolled in a night class. The committee will see his writing in the application anyway. Raising it yourself and pairing it with what he's doing about it helps him, and it makes the rest of your letter more believable.
5. **Close with what you'll back with action**, such as rehiring him or adjusting his schedule. Then your contact details.

## What to leave out, and one trap

- **Leave out the 5-week leave as a story.** A family medical emergency says nothing about his conduct, and the committee has no reason to ask about it.
- **The trap:** "missed 2 shifts in 3 years" would be misleading if you state it flat while leaving out the 5 weeks. You have two honest options:
  - Say something like "two missed shifts in three years, apart from one family medical leave, after which he came straight back to his shift."
  - Drop the number and rely on the 3 a.m. shift to show reliability.
  
  The first is stronger if he did come straight back, because it shows he returns to his commitments. Check with him that he's comfortable with it being mentioned.
- **Leave out the 2 days' notice.** Unless he actually had more warning than that, it isn't relevant.
- **Leave out where his family lives and anything about nationality.** It doesn't help the case, and it gives a reader a chance to be biased.
- **Keep "since he was 19"** but say it briefly. Starting young and staying three years is a point in his favour.

## Find out first (most useful first)

1. **What the college actually asks of referees.** Is there a form or set questions, a deadline, a named person to address it to? If there's a form, the letter should answer its questions in order.
2. **Will you rehire him, or let him work flexible hours while he studies?** If yes, this is the best answer you have to "will he finish." It's also a fact you'd have to commit to, so only include it if you mean it.
3. **Numbers for the mastitis cases.** How does nine compare with earlier winters? Roughly what did catching them early save in discarded milk, treatment, or cows that didn't have to be culled? Would your vet confirm it, or add a line of their own?
4. **How he learned the software.** Did he teach himself, ask to learn it, or show others how to use it? "He asked to learn it" shows initiative. "He now trains others" says even more.
5. **One specific night on the 3 a.m. shift** when something went wrong and he dealt with it alone. One concrete example will do more than general praise.
6. **Talk to Tomás** about three things:
   - what he plans to do after the program, so your letter supports his own statement
   - whether he's fine with you mentioning the English class and the leave
   - how far along the night class is.

Also note how many workers you've supervised over your career. "One of the best I've had in X years" only means something if you give the X.

Once you have these, the letter fits on one page without any filler.

## The angle

**You're the neighbor who'll live with the traffic, and you still support it.** That's the most useful thing you bring tonight. The commission will hear plenty of general arguments for affordable housing. What it rarely hears is someone two blocks away, who drives Alder every day, saying the downside is real and worth it. Opponents will probably be your neighbors talking about traffic. You're the one person in the room who can take that issue away from them.

So don't make it a general case for affordable housing. Make it a local case from someone with something at stake.

## Order (about 3 minutes, roughly 375–400 words spoken)

1. **Who you are (20 sec).** 19 years on Alder Road, two blocks from the site. Say that you're a member of the church right away. If an opponent points it out later, it looks like you hid it. If you say it first, it's just a fact.
2. **The need, made human (45 sec).** Use the waitlist (412 households, 3-year average wait) more than the 2,100 shortfall. 412 is local and concrete. Turn "60% AMI" into a real income and real jobs. Something like "a family of four earning under $X: classroom aides, CNAs, line cooks" lands far better than a percentage.
3. **The site makes sense (30 sec).** The land is already paved and sits empty 6 days a week. No green space is lost and no one is displaced.
4. **The tradeoff, faced directly (60 sec).** This is the core of the speech. Say plainly that the study shows 310 more trips and the Alder/4th intersection going from C to D at rush hour. Then explain why you accept that, using the facts you'll gather below. Do this before anyone else raises it.
5. **The ask (20 sec).** Ask for the specific action on tonight's agenda, such as approve, recommend approval, or grant the permit. Give one closing line about what the project means for the street you live on.

## Leave out

- **Most of your numbers.** Pick three or four. A list of statistics in a 3-minute slot blurs together, and the commissioners already have the reports.
- **The 2,100 figure, or use it carefully.** 64 units is about 3% of the shortfall, and an opponent can turn it into "this barely matters." If you use it at all, frame it as one step.
- **Religious or moral framing.** The commission decides based on land-use criteria. Leave mission language for church settings.
- **Any jabs at opponents or guesses about their motives.** You're their neighbor, and being generous makes you more credible.
- **The parking ratio (72 spaces for 64 units),** unless you know the county requirement and it compares well.

## Find out before tonight

1. **What exactly the commission is deciding** (rezoning, conditional use permit, site plan, or a recommendation to the board) and **what criteria it legally has to apply.** The staff report will say. Speak to those criteria.
2. **The county's adopted traffic standard for that intersection.** Many jurisdictions accept a D rating at peak hour. If yours does, "the intersection stays within the county's own standard" is your best line on traffic.
3. **Peak-hour trip numbers.** 310 a day sounds big. The rush-hour figure in the study is probably much smaller and more meaningful.
4. **Any traffic mitigation** in the study (signal timing, a turn lane) and whether the developer has committed to it.
5. **The county's parking requirement,** and where churchgoers will park on Sundays once apartments take the lot. Expect someone to ask.
6. **How long affordability is guaranteed** (15, 30, 55 years?) and who manages the property. Commissioners often ask about both.
7. **The 60% AMI income limit in dollars** for one person and a family of four.
8. **Whether there's a local or waitlist preference,** so you know if you can honestly say people on the county list could live there.
9. **Logistics:** how speaker sign-up works, whether written comments are accepted (send one anyway), and the time limit and speaking order.
10. **The opposition's main arguments,** from earlier meetings, letters in the staff report, or neighborhood groups, so you're answering their real objections.

If you can get #1, #2, and #7, you'll have most of what you need. Once you have them, send them back and the draft will come together quickly.

# Your angle for tonight

**Your best asset is that you live on Alder Road.** The commissioners will probably hear neighbors say the traffic on Alder will get worse. You can stand up as someone who will sit in that traffic, and has for 19 years, and still say the project is worth it. That's harder to dismiss than a general plea about the housing shortage. It also gets more weight if you say plainly what the project will cost you rather than skipping over it.

**Write for the commissioners, not for the opponents in the room.** The commission decides, and it decides on land-use grounds: does the project meet the county's standards, and does it serve the county's adopted goals? Use the county's own numbers and standards wherever you can, and keep moral or faith-based framing out.

## The argument in brief

- **What you're asking for:** approve the project (or recommend approval, depending on what tonight's vote actually is).
- **What it rests on:** the county's own report says it's short about 2,100 affordable homes. 412 households are waiting about three years. This lot is already paved and sits empty six days a week.
- **The link that makes those facts lead to approval:** when a project meets a need the county has documented, on land that's already paved and mostly unused, and its effects stay within the county's standards, it should be approved.
- **What could undercut it:** the traffic grade, parking, Sunday use of the lot, and the fact that you belong to the church.

## Order (3 minutes is about 375–400 words)

1. **Who you are and what you're asking, in the first 20 seconds.** You've lived on Alder for 19 years, two blocks from the church, and you're asking them to approve. Say that you belong to the church here. Someone else will point it out if you don't, and it sounds worse coming from them.
2. **The need, in the county's own figures.** 2,100 homes short; 412 households waiting about three years. Then 64 homes for households under 60% of area median income, built on a lot that's empty six days a week.
3. **The cost, stated by the person who'll pay it.** About 310 more trips a day on Alder, and Alder and 4th dropping from C to D at rush hour. Say both numbers yourself, then give your judgment as a neighbor: it's worth it. This is the part that makes your testimony count.
4. **Parking.** 72 spaces for 64 units, plus whatever answers the Sunday question (see below).
5. **What happens if they say no.** The 412 households keep waiting, the 2,100 shortfall stays where it is, and the lot stays empty six days a week. They'll weigh the project against doing nothing, so make sure doing nothing has a visible cost.
6. **Repeat the ask** in one sentence.

## What to find out before tonight (most important first)

1. **What exactly is the vote** (rezoning, special use permit, site plan)? **And did county staff recommend approval?** That tells you which standards to speak to. If staff recommended approval, say so; it's your strongest outside support.
2. **What grade is the county's adopted standard for that intersection?** Many counties accept D for busy intersections at peak hour. If yours does, "the intersection stays within the county's own standard" is the strongest thing you can say about traffic. If it doesn't, you need to know whether the developer has proposed a fix, such as signal timing or a turn lane.
3. **Where will Sunday worshippers park** once the lot has buildings on it? "Empty six days a week" invites the obvious follow-up about the seventh. If there's an answer (a shared-parking deal, a remaining lot, off-street spaces), use it. If there isn't, don't lead with the "six days" line.
4. **Does 72 spaces meet or beat the county's parking requirement** for this kind of housing? If it does, say it in one line.
5. **Will these units actually be filled from the housing authority's waiting list?** Don't say "64 families come off the list" unless that's true. The list and these units may work differently. If you can't confirm it, use the waitlist only as evidence of need.
6. **Who will build and run it, and for how many years are rents restricted?** An experienced affordable-housing operator and a long restriction period answer the unspoken worry about whether this will be run well and stay affordable. One sentence is enough.

## What to leave out

- **Faith or mission language**, and any line about the church "doing the right thing." It isn't a land-use reason, and it pushes the conversation toward who is building it instead of whether it meets the standards.
- **Saying the traffic numbers are small.** You can't back that up, and someone who lives there will push back. Concede them and weigh them instead.
- **Answers to opponents by name**, or any guessing at their motives.
- **Descriptions of the future tenants that sound like charity** ("these poor families"). Under 60% of median income covers a lot of working people, and if your county publishes the 60% figure for a household, one example can work well. Only use it if you look it up.
- **The 3% math.** Yes, 64 is a small share of 2,100, but don't do the division for them. "This is 64 of the 2,100 the county says it needs" is enough.

## A second audience

The traffic and parking objections come from your neighbors, and persuading them is a different speech with different points to make (for example, how construction and parking will be managed, and who residents can call). Tonight, speak only to the commission. If the project goes forward, you're well placed to make that case to neighbors later.

Once you have answers to the first three questions, the speech will mostly write itself.

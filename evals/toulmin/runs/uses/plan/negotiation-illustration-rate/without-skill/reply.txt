## The angle

**You're asking for a raise that's overdue, and it's still below what the market pays you.** You've been at $9,000 for 6 books over 4 years, you have proven sales, and $11,500 sits in the middle of your agent's range and $1,000 under what another publisher paid you last year. Present it as a reasonable correction from someone who wants to keep working with them, not as a threat.

## Leave out: "every other publisher pays at least $12,000"

Your own facts don't support that claim, and it's the kind of thing an editor can check.

- You have one comparable fee ($12,500), not "every other publisher."
- Your agent's range starts at $11,000, which is below $12,000. If the editor asks your agent or another illustrator, the claim falls apart.
- An overstated claim gives them an easy reason to push back on the whole email. Your real numbers are strong enough without it.

Use the true version: another publisher paid you $12,500 last year for a book of the same length, and your agent puts the going rate at $11,000 to $13,000.

## Order

1. **Warm opening and a yes.** You're glad to be offered the book and want to do it. This makes clear you're negotiating, not leaving.
2. **The track record, briefly.** 6 books, two over 20,000 copies, one on a state reading list. These results made them money, so put them first.
3. **The flat fee.** Your rate hasn't changed since your first book with them, while your record has grown.
4. **The market evidence.** The $12,500 comparable and your agent's range, stated plainly.
5. **The specific ask.** $11,500 for this book, given as a single number, not a range. If you give a range, they'll pick the bottom.
6. **Collaborative close.** You're happy to talk it through and look forward to the project.

## Probably leave out

- **The 4 months per book.** It's true, but it makes the argument about your effort rather than your value, and they already know how long the work takes.
- **Any ultimatum.** Unless you're truly willing to walk away, don't suggest it.
- **The other publisher's name.** "Another publisher" is enough. Naming them can come across as a breach of confidence.

## Find out first

- **Is your agent handling this?** If you have an agent, publishers usually expect fee talks to go through them. Check whether your agent should send it, or at least knows you're sending it. This could change who signs the email.
- **What will you accept?** Decide your floor before you send. If they counter at $10,500, will you take it? Knowing this keeps you from deciding under pressure.
- **What else could you trade?** If they can't move much on the fee, would a higher royalty, a bigger advance against royalties, a longer schedule, or a raise built into the next book be worth it to you? Have one or two ready.
- **How is the contract set up?** Is the $9,000 a flat fee or an advance against royalties? If those 20,000+ copy books earn royalties, it changes both the argument and what you can ask for.
- **Timing.** Check whether they need an answer by a set date, so the email doesn't land too late.

At under 250 words, there's room for the six steps above with one or two sentences each, which is about right for this email.

## The angle

**You're asking them to bring a proven illustrator up to market rate, and you're asking for less than you've already been paid elsewhere.** The email should feel like a loyal collaborator making an overdue adjustment, not a threat to leave.

The step from your facts to your ask is simple: an illustrator whose books sell and win placements should be paid what the market pays for that record. Your editor probably already agrees with that, so you don't need to spell it out. What you need is facts they can't dispute.

## What it should say, in this order

1. **You want the book.** Open by saying you're glad to be offered it. That makes clear this is a negotiation, not a breakup.
2. **Your record with them.** Six books in four years. Two have sold over 20,000 copies, and one made a state reading list. Name the titles and the list. They already know these numbers, so this just reminds them.
3. **The fee hasn't changed.** It has been $9,000 since your first book. Say it once, plainly, without complaint.
4. **The market.** Another publisher paid you $12,500 last year for a book of the same length. Your agent puts the normal range for your record at $11,000–13,000.
5. **The ask, as a figure.** Write "my fee for this book is $11,500," not "I'd like." Then point out that this is $1,000 below what you were just paid elsewhere, because you value working with them. That's your strongest line, and it turns the ask into a concession.
6. **Close warmly.** Say you're ready to start and happy to talk it over.

## What to leave out

- **"Every other publisher pays at least $12,000."** Your facts don't support it. You have one publisher at $12,500, and your agent's range starts at $11,000, which is below $12,000. An editor can check this or simply doubt it. If they catch one overstatement, they'll discount everything else in the email. The true version is stronger anyway: "I was paid $12,500 for a comparable book last year."
- **Ultimatums or hints that you might leave.** Save that for a second round if they refuse.
- **The four months per book**, unless you want to point out that $9,000 works out to about $2,250 a month. That figure can read as a grievance, so I'd leave it out.
- **Your fallback number.** Never put it in the email.

## Find out first

- **Should your agent send this, or be copied on it?** If they negotiate your contracts, a direct email from you can undercut them.
- **Can you cite the $12,500?** Check that contract for confidentiality terms. You may need to drop the publisher's name, or the figure itself.
- **Is the $9,000 a flat fee or an advance against royalties?** If it's an advance and the 20,000-copy books have earned out, that's a strong extra point: they've already made money on you.
- **Who approves the fee?** If your editor has to take it to someone else, write it so they can forward it as it stands. That person is your real reader.
- **Your walk-away point and other options.** Decide in advance what you'd accept short of $11,500: a different number, a higher royalty, a longer schedule, or a promise of $11,500 on the next book.
- **Exact sales figures, titles, the list's name, and when they need an answer.**

At five or six short paragraphs, this fits comfortably under 250 words.

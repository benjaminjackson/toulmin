# Grant narrative plan: angle, order, and what to check first

## The angle

**Farms in your area are already offering you fresh food, and you're turning it down because you can't keep it cold.** That's your strongest argument. You don't need to prove the food exists, because it's already being offered. The van is what gets it from the farm to your 610 households. You're also paying 26% of the cost yourselves, so the foundation isn't carrying all the risk. Build the whole narrative around that.

## Find out before writing

**1. The "double our fresh food" claim. Your numbers don't support it yet.**
- You give out 6,000 lb of fresh food a month, which is about 72,000 lb a year. Doubling means getting another 72,000 lb.
- Your proof so far is 9,000 lb turned down last summer. That covers about one-eighth of what doubling requires.
- A reviewer will do this math. If the headline claim looks inflated, they'll doubt everything else in the narrative.
- You can do either of these:
  - Find the other sources: grocery store rescue, dairy, cold pickups from your regional food bank, or farms with fall and winter crops. Then add up a realistic total for the year.
  - Or drop the doubling claim. "An estimated X additional pounds a year, starting with the 9,000 lb we turned away last summer" is more believable.

**2. Cold storage.** You said you turned the produce down because you couldn't pick it up cold **or store it**. The van fixes pickup only. Do you have enough cooler and fridge space for the extra food? If not, the foundation will ask, and you need an answer.

**3. Running costs.** Estimate the yearly cost of insurance, fuel, maintenance, registration and upkeep of the refrigeration unit, and say who pays. This matters more because of your reserves (point 4).

**4. Your reserves after the purchase.** You'd have $11,000 left. Work out how many months of operating costs that covers. If it's thin, have a plan, such as rebuilding reserves or a sponsor for running costs. Don't let the reviewer be the first to notice.

**5. Drivers.**
- Check that your insurer will cover volunteer drivers. Insurers often set age or driving-record rules.
- Check whether the van's size or weight means drivers need a special license.
- Four drivers is a good number. A sample weekly schedule makes it believable.

**6. Letters from the farms.** Get short letters from the farms saying what they'd donate next season. That turns last summer's offers into promises for next season, and it's the strongest evidence you can attach.

**7. People served.** 610 households is probably 1,400 to 1,600 people, roughly one in six people in town. Get your real headcount and use it. That share is a strong number.

**8. The van quote.** Note whether the van is new or used, and how long you expect it to last. Over 10 years, $50,000 works out to a very low cost per pound of food, and that's worth including.

**9. The application rules.** Check whether vehicles are eligible and whether there's a separate section for past grants.

## Order (about 500 words)

1. **The problem, in one picture (about 75 words):** 9,000 lb of local produce turned away last summer because you couldn't keep it cold.
2. **Who you serve (about 75 words):** 610 households, the share of the town, and that only 16% of what you hand out is fresh (6,000 of 38,000 lb).
3. **The fix (about 100 words):** The van, which farms it will reach, the four drivers and their weekly schedule, and where the extra food will be stored.
4. **What changes (about 100 words):** A realistic target in pounds, and how you'll measure it. Pounds of fresh food logged each month is enough.
5. **Money and long-term costs (about 75 words):** Your $18,000 share, the yearly running costs, and who pays them.
6. **Track record (about 75 words):** What the 2023 shelving grant achieved, and one sentence on the late report.

## What to leave out or handle carefully

- **Doubling:** leave it out unless you can show where the food comes from (point 1).
- **The late report:** don't hide it. The foundation already knows. One plain sentence is enough: it was late, here's what you changed (for example, a named person now owns reporting and deadlines are on a calendar), and you'll file on time. Saying it yourself builds more trust than leaving it out.
- **The $11,000 reserve figure:** don't put it in the narrative by itself. Show it in the budget alongside your plan for it (point 4).
- **"23 volunteers":** cut it if you're short on words. The four committed drivers are the number that matters.
- **General hunger statistics:** skip them. Your local numbers are stronger.

If points 1 and 2 hold up, the rest of the narrative is straightforward.

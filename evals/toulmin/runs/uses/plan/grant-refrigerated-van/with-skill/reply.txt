## The angle

**Local farms are already offering you fresh food, and you're turning it away because you can't keep it cold.** That's the strongest thing you have. The foundation doesn't need persuading that hunger matters. What will move them is that their money unlocks food that already exists in the county, from local farms, for local households, and that you're paying more than a quarter of the cost yourselves.

The argument underneath it, which you probably don't need to state: when the only thing between donated food and the families who need it is cold transport, paying for the transport is a cheap way to feed more people. A community foundation will accept that. Your job is to prove the "only thing" part.

## The doubling claim doesn't hold up yet

- **Doubling** means going from about 6,000 to 12,000 lb of fresh food a month. That's about 72,000 extra pounds in year one.
- **The evidence you have** is 9,000 lb offered over three summer months, or about 3,000 lb a month for one season. That covers about one eighth of what doubling needs. Even at the peak of summer it's a 50% increase, not 100%.

A program officer can do this arithmetic. If they do, the overstated claim will make them doubt the rest of the narrative. You have two options:

1. **Find more evidence.** If the van would also bring in grocery-store dairy and eggs, more from a regional food bank, or produce outside the summer months, and you can name those sources, doubling may hold up.
2. **Narrow the claim to something they can check.** For example: "In the first year we'll collect at least the 9,000 lb we refused last summer, and report the pounds collected by [date]." You'd state a longer-term goal separately as a goal. The target and date have to be ones you can actually meet.

Your choice, but don't submit "double" as it stands.

## Suggested order (about 500 words)

1. **The food you turned down.** 9,000 lb, local farms, last summer, and why you had to refuse it.
2. **Who you serve.** 610 households, 38,000 lb a month, of which only 6,000 lb is fresh.
3. **Your history with the foundation.** What the 2023 shelving grant made possible. Then the late report, stated once and plainly, with what has changed since. Put this before the ask. The program officer probably remembers it, and raising it yourself builds more trust than hoping they've forgotten.
4. **The ask and your share.** $68,000 van, $50,000 requested, $18,000 from your reserves.
5. **What changes, with a checkpoint.** Whichever claim you settle on, plus what you'll report and when.
6. **How the van will run.** Four committed drivers on a weekly schedule, cold storage at your site, and who pays running costs.
7. **What happens if they say no.** Next summer's produce gets turned away again.

## Find out before writing

1. **Can you store the extra food cold?** Last summer you had no way to pick it up cold *or* store it. A van solves the first problem, not necessarily the second. If you don't have the fridge space, the case falls apart.
2. **Who pays to run the van?** Insurance, fuel, maintenance and upkeep of the refrigeration unit. With only $11,000 left in reserves, a reviewer will ask. If you know your monthly operating costs, say how many months $11,000 covers.
3. **Will the farms commit again?** Letters from them promising the same or more next year would be your best evidence. Getting those letters is a separate task: you're persuading the farms, not the foundation.
4. **Why buy instead of renting a refrigerated truck for summer?** Expect this question. A year-round need is the best answer, which ties back to the doubling question.
5. **What changed after the late report?** For example, a named person now handles reporting, or a calendar system. You need a real answer, not a promise you can't keep.
6. **What does the shelving grant show?** Any measurable result, such as more storage capacity or more food distributed, shows you deliver on grants.

Also check the foundation's scoring criteria and whether the budget goes in a separate section. If it does, the narrative can spend fewer words on numbers.

## Leave out

- National food-insecurity statistics. They use up words and the foundation already knows them.
- Any cost-per-pound figure based only on the 9,000 lb. It makes the van look expensive.
- The total count of 23 volunteers, unless there's space. The four drivers are the number that matters here.

Don't leave out the late report, the reserve balance or the running costs. The reviewer will find them anyway, and it's better that they read them from you along with your answer.

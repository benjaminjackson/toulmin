# Planning the open letter about the elevator

## Who you're writing for

The letter is addressed to the management company, but three groups will read it, and they need different things:

- **Management** is the only group that can act, so the asks have to be specific enough to say yes or no to.
- **TV viewers and the station** will judge whether your complaint is reasonable. A reporter will call management for comment, so every figure has to hold up.
- **Your neighbours in the lobby** need to see the association being firm and accurate.

Write for the TV viewer. If a neutral stranger thinks your asks are fair, management can't brush them off.

Also check whether management actually decides. If they act for an owner, the owner pays for repairs and a second elevator. The owner may be the real reader and should get a copy.

## The angle

**Lead with reliability and broken promises, not the second elevator.** Here is the argument in one sentence: when the only elevator in a 9-storey building is out about one day in eight, residents who can't use stairs are stuck. Management has given the same "6 to 8 weeks" answer twice in five months, so it needs to make firm, written commitments now.

Why not lead with the second elevator?

- It's your weakest point. Management already has a cost ($400,000) and a reason it can't be done.
- If you lead with it, the story becomes "tenants want an expensive elevator," and you lose.
- The reliability case is very hard to argue with:
  - 41 days out so far this year is roughly 13% of the days.
  - The 12 days in August fell in the hottest month.
  - "6 to 8 weeks" was promised in June, so the part should have arrived by mid-August. It's now mid-November.

Keep the second elevator, but change what you ask for. Don't demand it. Ask management to **show its work**: the written basis for "can't be done," or an independent feasibility study. Mention the 74 households (two-thirds of the building) to show the request has broad support.

## The objection to answer up front

Management will say the city passed the elevator in September. Don't argue with the inspection. Agree with it, then point out what it doesn't cover. **A certificate says the elevator is safe when it runs. It says nothing about how often it runs.** One calm sentence like this takes away their best reply.

## Suggested order (about 400 words)

1. **What it means for people (about 60 words).** In a 9-storey building with one elevator, 6 residents who use wheelchairs or walkers can't leave home or get back in when it's down. Mention the 23 residents over 75.
2. **The record (about 80 words).** 41 days, the 12-day stretch in August, and the June promise repeated now.
3. **The inspection (about 40 words).** Agree it passed and explain why that doesn't answer the problem.
4. **Specific, dated asks (about 120 words).** This is the core of the letter. Only include asks the association has agreed on, for example:
   - written proof the part was ordered and a delivery date
   - a service contract with guaranteed response times
   - what help residents get during outages, such as stair assistance or notice
   - the basis for the claim that a second elevator can't be built
5. **The petition (about 40 words).** 74 of 112 households, framed as a request for information, not a demand.
6. **Reply deadline and next step (about 60 words).** Give a date. Only name next steps the association will really take, such as a complaint to the city housing department or a letter to your council member.

## What to leave out

- **Residents' names and health details** unless each person agrees in writing. Ask willing residents to speak to the reporter themselves.
- **Guesses about motive**, such as "stalling" or "putting profit first." Stick to facts you can document; that also protects the association legally.
- **"Dangerous" or "unsafe"**, unless you have an actual incident. The inspection result would undercut you.
- **Threats** like a rent strike or lawsuit that the association hasn't voted on.
- **Any rebuttal of the $400,000 figure** unless you have your own estimate.

## What to find out first

1. **Can you document the 41 days?** Is there a log with dates, and were they full days? A reporter will ask, and so will management.
2. **Is the June promise in writing?** An email or notice is much stronger than "they told us."
3. **Did any harm happen?** Someone carried down by EMS, a missed medical appointment, someone stuck upstairs for days. One true, consented example is worth more than any number.
4. **What floors are the 6 mobility-impaired residents on?** "Lives on the 8th floor" makes the problem concrete.
5. **Is the elevator working right now, and has it failed since September?** An outage after the inspection strengthens your case.
6. **What does the law require?** Your city's housing code or lease may set repair timelines, and fair-housing law covers accommodations for disabled tenants. A local tenant-rights group can tell you quickly. If there's a clear obligation, it becomes your strongest point.
7. **Who owns the building**, and does management have authority to approve major repairs?
8. **Has the association voted on the text, the asks and who signs?** You're the secretary; the letter should go out under the association's name.

**One tactical point:** consider sending the letter to management a few days before you post it or contact the station. The reporter will ask whether you gave them a chance to respond. Being able to say "yes, and they didn't" makes you look more reasonable.

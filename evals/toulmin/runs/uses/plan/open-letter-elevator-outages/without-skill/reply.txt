## The angle

**Lead with the broken promise.** In June management said the part would arrive in 6 to 8 weeks. It's now mid-November, roughly 22 weeks later, and they're giving the same estimate. That's your story. It's simple, it can be checked, and it's about accountability, so nobody has to judge engineering claims.

The frame: **"One elevator, 112 apartments, and the same promise twice."** The problem is that you depend on a single elevator that keeps failing, and nobody is being straight with you about when it will be fixed.

## Suggested order

1. **The promise.** June: "6–8 weeks." November: "6–8 weeks."
2. **The stakes.** 9 floors, one elevator, 41 days out this year, including 12 straight days in August. 23 residents over 75 and 6 who use wheelchairs or walkers. One short, real example of the impact, used with that person's consent, will do more than the statistics.
3. **The mandate.** 74 of 112 households (66%) signed. Use it to show the association speaks for the building.
4. **Concrete asks with a deadline.** For example:
   - Written proof the part has been ordered: supplier, order date, confirmed delivery date.
   - An outage plan for now: advance notice, help for residents who can't use the stairs, and a contact who answers.
   - Rent credits for outage days, if your local law supports them.
   - A meeting with the association by a specific date.
5. **Close** with who signed and how to reach you.

## Make the second elevator a smaller ask

It's what people signed for, but as the headline demand it hands management an easy reply: "$400,000, structurally impossible, unrealistic tenants." Mention the petition as evidence of how serious the problem is. Then ask for an **independent feasibility assessment** or a long-term reliability plan, not the elevator itself.

## Leave out

- **Any suggestion the elevator is unsafe.** It passed city inspection in September, and a reporter will find that in five minutes. Your point is reliability, not safety. Consider getting ahead of it in one line: "It passed inspection. It still doesn't run."
- **Guesses about motives** ("they don't care," "they're lying"). State the facts and let readers draw the conclusion. This also protects you legally.
- **Health details or names** of residents without their explicit consent, especially since this is going to TV.

## Find out first

- **Your outage records.** Who logged the 41 days, and do you have dates? A TV station will ask.
- **The June promise in writing.** An email or notice is much stronger than "they told us."
- **Whether the part was actually ordered and is really backordered.** Call the elevator maintenance contractor or the manufacturer. If normal lead time is 3 weeks, your story gets much stronger. If there's a real shortage, adjust your tone.
- **What caused the August outage.** Was it the same controller problem? If so, that shows a pattern.
- **Local law.** Many cities and states have elevator-service rules, habitability standards or rent-abatement rights. Ask a tenant-rights group or legal aid. Also check whether your area has protections against landlord retaliation.
- **City complaint records.** Have outages been reported to the city's building department or 311?
- **Internal sign-off.** As secretary, get the board or membership to approve both the text and the decision to go to the media.
- **Sequencing.** Consider sending the letter to management first with a short response deadline, then going to the TV station if they don't respond. A brush-off is itself a story, and you'll look reasonable.
- **Lobby posting rules** in your lease or building rules.

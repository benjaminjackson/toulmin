# Angle for Priya's PhD recommendation letter

**Recommended angle:** Priya is already a field researcher, not just a strong student. She designed and carried out a 14-month study largely on her own, and she has done paid field work. The 90 kayak trips are your best evidence, so build the letter around them.

## Change the "top journal" claim first

I'd advise against saying her findings are ready for a top journal. The study has six sites over one season, no repeat, and a three-month gap at one site. PhD faculty in marine ecology will see that right away. An inflated claim makes them discount everything else you say, including the true and impressive parts.

Here's a version that's strong and still defensible: her results are a promising first finding. The salt-marsh pattern deserves follow-up, and with another season of data it could support a publication. That shows she's already producing questions worth a PhD. If a manuscript is actually in progress, say so plainly instead.

## Order (one page, about 5 short paragraphs)

1. **Opening and bottom line.** How you know her (3 courses plus thesis advisor) and your experience (17 years, about 30 theses). Then rank her directly, for example "among the top X of the thesis students I've supervised." Committees value that comparison above almost anything else.
2. **The thesis.** The question, the six sites, 14 months, about 90 kayak trips, and the salt-marsh result. Include how she handled the storm data loss. Dealing well with a setback like that is strong evidence of research maturity.
3. **Outside validation.** The paid summer job at the state shellfish lab and the regional poster. Together these show other people trusted her work and she can present it.
4. **Classroom record.** Top grade in a class of 28 in two of your courses. Keep it to one or two sentences.
5. **Close.** Why marine ecology PhD work suits her, and an unreserved endorsement.

## What to leave out

- The "top journal" language
- The third course's grade, unless it was also strong
- Any repetition of what her CV already lists. Your letter should explain the facts, not restate them.

## What to find out first

- **The storm gap:** Did she adjust her analysis, flag the gap, or change the design? This could become your best paragraph.
- **How independent she was:** Was the question her idea? Did she do the statistics herself?
- **Her actual rank** among your roughly 30 thesis students.
- **The shellfish lab:** Anything her supervisor there said, and what her duties were.
- **The poster:** Any reaction, questions, or award.
- **Her plans:** Which programs or advisors she's targeting and what her statement of purpose says, so the letter supports it. Ask her for the CV and statement.
- **One anecdote:** A specific moment showing persistence or curiosity, such as a bad-weather trip or a question she raised that you hadn't thought of.
- **Any manuscript plans:** Whether she's planning a second season or a paper.

Once you have those answers, the letter should be easy to draft.

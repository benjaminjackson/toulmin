## The angle

**The letter should argue that Priya is ready to do independent field research at PhD level, not that her thesis is ready for a top journal.**

The admissions committee is judging her, not her thesis. Your strongest facts are about how she works:

- She did 14 months of fieldwork on her own.
- That was about 90 kayak trips, roughly one every 4 to 5 days, through all seasons.
- She worked six sites and kept the study going after a storm.
- A state lab then chose to pay her to do field work.

That is what a marine ecology advisor most wants to know about an applicant: will this person keep going when the fieldwork is hard, the weather is bad, and things break? Your facts answer that directly.

## Why I'd drop "ready for a top journal"

The readers are marine ecologists, and they will test that sentence against what the letter itself says:

- **Only one year of data.** One year can't separate a site effect from a good or bad year.
- **Only two marsh sites.** "Near the marsh" may also mean different salinity, water flow, temperature or food. With two sites you can't tell which is causing the faster growth.
- **One site lost three months of data.**
- **A poster isn't peer review.**

A reader who doubts that sentence will start doubting the rest of the letter, and your 17 years and 30 theses are what give the letter its weight. You can still make a real claim about the work. For example: "the thesis is a strong basis for a publication," or "with a second season, this is publishable," or "we are preparing it for [named journal]." Use only the version you can stand behind. Which one is your call, but I'd strongly advise against the top-journal wording.

## Order on the page

1. **Who you are, and your ranking of her.** Give the 17 years and 30 theses, then a ranking such as "among the top X students I've supervised." Committees value a ranking more than anything else in a letter, and putting it first makes it count.
2. **The thesis, told as how she worked.** Cover who came up with the question, the 90 trips, how she dealt with the storm gap, and the marsh finding stated as what one year showed. Mentioning that it's one year of data in passing makes you more believable, not less.
3. **The summer job at the state shellfish lab.** This shows that someone outside your college judged her worth paying.
4. **Coursework.** Keep it short: top of a class of 28 in two of her three courses with you.
5. **The poster.** One line.
6. **Close.** Why she fits marine ecology and these programs, and a recommendation without reservation.

## What to leave out

- The top-journal claim.
- General praise with no fact behind it ("brilliant", "passionate").
- The third course, unless her grade there helps.

## What to find out first

1. **Where does she rank among your ~30 thesis students?** This could become the first sentence of the letter.
2. **What was hers and what was yours?** Who chose the question, designed the study and ran the statistics? And what did she do when the storm wiped out three months at one site? If she handled it well, it's one of your best examples.
3. **What did the shellfish lab think of her?** Was she asked back? Did her supervisor praise specific work? Is that supervisor writing a letter too?
4. **What's the actual publication plan?** A second season, a target journal, co-authors? Your claim about publishing should match whatever this is.
5. **What is she telling the programs herself?** If her statement of purpose calls the thesis "in prep" or names a journal, your letter needs to agree with it. Also: which programs or advisors is she applying to, and does any of them work on marshes or shellfish?
6. **Is there anything the committee will see anyway?** A weak grade, a gap, a skill she still needs (statistics, for example)? It's better to address it briefly in your letter than to leave readers to find it on their own.

## The angle

**"You'll know who's coming, and it's worth paying a bit more for."**

Your two main exit reasons are linked. People who pay $25 more than the app services expect consistency. When a different cleaner showed up, the higher price stopped feeling worth it. So don't try to win on price, because you'll lose that. Instead, show what the $165 buys now that the cleaner problem is fixed, and use the $99 offer to make coming back to try it low-risk.

## Don't promise the same cleaner every time

You can't keep that promise, and these customers have already seen why. With 7 of 18 cleaners leaving last year, and sickness, vacations and scheduling on top of that, it will break. The people most likely to notice are the ones who left because of this exact problem. Breaking the same promise twice is worse than never winning them back.

What you can honestly promise is better anyway: **a regular cleaner, plus a named backup you've met, so you'll never open the door to a stranger.** It's specific and believable, and it fixes the actual complaint, which was strangers showing up rather than the cleaner changing in itself.

## Suggested order (~150 words)

1. **Admit the problem (1–2 sentences).** "You told us the cleaner kept changing. You were right." Owning it builds trust, and they already know.
2. **What changed:** a regular cleaner and a named backup. Say it concretely.
3. **Why the price is what it is (one line):** cleaners are employees, not contractors, and every visit is insured. Frame this as what they get: accountability and safety in their home.
4. **The offer:** first cleaning back for $99, then the regular price.
5. **One clear next step:** reply, call, or a booking link.

## Leave out

- **The turnover numbers** (7 of 18, 4 in 10). They're true, but they remind readers of the problem without proving it's fixed.
- **The competitor's $140 price.** Naming it starts a price comparison you lose.
- **The $21/hour wage, probably.** "Employees, not gig workers" makes the point. A specific wage invites people to do the math on your margin. One exception: if you can honestly say pay changes cut turnover, that's a strong line.
- **"Every single time" or any absolute wording.**

## Find out first

- **Has turnover improved?** How many cleaners have left since you started the backup system, and when did it start? If retention is better, one honest sentence like "our team has been stable since spring" is your strongest proof.
- **Is each customer's old cleaner still with you?** If so, "Maria is still here and has an opening on Tuesdays" will beat any general email. With only 64 people, personalizing is realistic.
- **Why did each person leave?** If you can split price leavers from cleaner leavers, send two versions. The cleaner group gets the consistency message first. The price group gets the $99 offer and the value case first.
- **Terms of the $99 offer:** Is there a deadline? Does it require booking a regular schedule afterward? Will they pay $165 after that, or their old rate? Customers will ask.
- **What happens when both the regular and the backup are out?** Decide this before you send the email. Someone will ask, and it's when the promise gets tested.

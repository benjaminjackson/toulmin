## The angle

**The promise you want to make is the one your facts can't support.** You can't promise "the same cleaner every single time." You have a regular cleaner plus a named backup, which means that some visits will be done by the backup. And 7 of your 18 cleaners left last year. If even one returning customer gets someone new, the email turns into proof that you break promises, and these are people who already left once over exactly that.

What you can promise is stronger than it sounds: **"You'll know who's coming. Your regular cleaner, or a backup we've named to you in advance. Never a stranger."** That fixes the actual complaint, which was surprise and churn, not the occasional sick day. You decide what to promise. I'd go with this one unless you can stand behind the absolute version.

**The argument:** you left for two reasons. We fixed one. The other one buys you something, and coming back to check costs $99.

## Suggested order (~150 words)

1. **Admit the cleaner problem first.** Don't start with a sales line. People who left over rotating cleaners need to hear that you know it happened. Skip the "4 in 10" figure; saying "we heard you" is enough.
2. **What changed:** a regular cleaner and a named backup.
3. **Name the price gap yourself.** Say you're about $25 more than the apps, then say what that buys: employees, not gig contractors, and insurance on every visit. If you say it first, it reads as honest. If they work it out themselves, it reads as hidden.
4. **The offer:** first cleaning back for $99, and say plainly that it's $165 after that. They'll learn the price anyway.
5. **One easy next step**, with a deadline if you have one.

## What to leave out

- The 7-of-18 turnover number and the 64 cancellations. They're true, but they don't help this reader decide, and they invite doubt.
- The $21/hour wage, unless you can link it to cleaners staying (see question 1 below). As it stands, your own turnover undercuts the idea that good pay keeps cleaners.
- Any claim about the app services (like "they send someone different every time") unless you actually know it's true.

## What to find out first

1. **Has turnover improved since you added the backup system, or raised pay?** If this year's numbers are better, that's the strongest support you have for the reliability promise. Without it, "we fixed it" is just a claim.
2. **Can a returning customer get their old cleaner back** if that person still works for you? "Maria is still here and has an opening" will win back more people than anything else you could write.
3. **What happens if a stranger shows up anyway?** For example, a free clean or a discount. That gives the promise weight. Only include it if you'll honour it.
4. **Will customers meet or hear from the backup ahead of time?** Without that, "named backup" is just a name.
5. **Does the $99 offer have an end date, and is $165 locked in afterwards?**
6. **Consider two versions.** The people who left over price and the ones who left over cleaner churn need different openings. If your exit surveys can split the list, two short emails will beat one blended one.

Once you have answers, especially to #1 and #2, the email is easy to write.

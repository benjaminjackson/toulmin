# Your grant summary: angle, order, and what to find out first

## The main problem: the money doesn't add up yet

A reviewer will do this math in a few seconds:

- A third attorney costs $78,000 a year.
- The foundation pays at most $45,000 a year, which is your $90,000 over two years.
- That leaves **$33,000 a year, or $66,000 over the grant**, with no stated source.

Then they'll see that one of your two attorneys leaves in June. The obvious question is whether this is really a third attorney or a replacement. Many funders won't pay to refill an existing position.

The summary has to answer both questions. Right now your facts don't. So the angle depends on two answers only you can get (see "Find out first" below).

## The angle

**Keep the claim narrow:** this grant adds one attorney, and that attorney means about 80 more tenants a year get a lawyer.

Your reviewers are lawyers with an access-to-justice mission. They already believe tenants facing eviction should have counsel, so you don't need to argue that. What they need is a clear picture of the gap, proof that you can deliver, and a plan that holds together. Your facts already give you most of that:

- **Need:** 1,140 eviction filings, and you represented the tenant in 163. That's about 14%.
- **Demand:** about 300 tenants turned away only because no attorney was free. That's more than the 163 you served.
- **Capacity:** about 80 open cases per attorney. A third attorney at that load serves roughly 80 more tenants a year, which would take you from about 14% to about 21% of filings.

Don't suggest the grant meets the whole need. Eighty more tenants a year covers about a quarter of the people you turned away. Saying that plainly makes you more credible, and it shows the need is bigger than the ask.

## Order (about 300 words)

1. **The gap** (about 60 words): filings, how many you represented, how many you turned away.
2. **What you do and what happens** (about 60 words): the 58% outcome, described plainly.
3. **The ask and what it buys** (about 60 words): $90,000 over two years for a third attorney, about 80 more tenants a year, start date.
4. **How the position is fully funded** (about 70 words): where the other $33,000 a year comes from, how the June departure is handled, and the county contract.
5. **After two years** (about 40 words): how the position continues.

Section 4 is the one most applicants skip. In your case it's the one reviewers will look for.

## What to leave out, or word carefully

- **Don't compare the 58% to tenants without lawyers.** You don't have that number, so don't imply it ("far better than…"). Present it as your outcome rate. If you can find a sourced comparison (see below), it becomes your strongest point.
- **Don't count the same attorney twice.** Either the new attorney serves more tenants, or they bring 80-case loads down. If loads stay at 80, the gain is about 80 tenants. If you plan to lower loads, the gain is smaller. Pick one and give the number.
- **Leave out general eviction statistics** unless you can cite them. With 300 words, your own numbers are stronger.
- **Don't leave out the departure or the funding gap.** Reviewers will see both in the budget or hear about them later. If you state them once, with the answer next to them, they'll trust everything else you wrote.

## Find out first

1. **Where the other $33,000 a year comes from.** Is it already committed (another grant, a donor, your reserves), or are you still raising it? The summary can say "committed" or "we are seeking," but not neither.
2. **How the June departure fits.** Who pays the departing attorney now, and do you plan to refill that job? If refilling it and adding the new attorney leaves you with three, say so. If the grant only gets you back to two, that's a different and weaker application, and it may run into rules against funding existing positions.
3. **Which October the county renewal means.** It's already November 2026. If the renewal just happened, say it was renewed (or report what changed). If it's October 2027, it falls inside the grant period, and you should say what happens if it isn't renewed. Either way, the county is a second audience: a foundation award could help your county renewal, and the reverse is also true.
4. **The foundation's rules:** Does it accept partial funding or require a match? Does it bar replacing existing staff? Does it ask for a plan to keep the position going after the grant?
5. **Local data on tenants without lawyers.** The court clerk or a court-data dashboard may have default and judgment rates for unrepresented tenants in your county. Even a default rate turns your 58% from a number with no comparison into a contrast.
6. **What your numbers count.** Is the 300 unique households or calls? Is the 58% out of closed cases only? How does it split between tenants who stayed and tenants who got 60+ days? Is 80 cases each a snapshot of open cases or a yearly total? Reviewers who are lawyers may ask, and the 80-more-tenants estimate depends on the last one.

Once you have answers to 1–3, the summary will be easy to write. If you send them, I'll go through the plan again before drafting.

Your angle is shared stakes, not a policy announcement. The email shouldn't read as "the partners want people back." It should say: we're losing work we should win, our newer people are growing more slowly than they should, and we're fixing it by being in the same room, starting with ourselves.

## The argument underneath

- **The decision:** from September 1, everyone is in the office Tuesday to Thursday.
- **The facts it rests on:**
  - Two hospital clients told you your team seemed not to know each other.
  - Juniors hired since 2022 are taking about 30% longer to lead a project.
  - Partners have been in one day a week.
- **The link between them:** in architecture, you learn the work and win it by working side by side. People only look like a team in an interview if they actually work as one. Staff will mostly accept this about mentoring. They'll accept it less readily about their own jobs, so don't spell it out at length. Let the two facts carry it.
- **Your strongest point is the partners' change.** It admits the partners were part of the problem, and it means you're taking on the same cost you're asking of everyone else. Say it plainly and early, not as a closing gesture.

## Order for the page

1. **The decision and the date, in the first two sentences.** Nobody should have to hunt for it.
2. **Why: the hospital losses.** It's an external, specific loss that everyone shared. Lead with this one because nobody will dispute it.
3. **Why: the juniors.** This is about their own careers, not about the firm's output. Word it as "we think being apart is part of this," not as proven cause (see below).
4. **What the partners are changing.** You'll be in all three days.
5. **The people hired as fully remote.** Say directly that they joined on different terms and this changes something you offered them. Explain the arrangement for those who live far away. Then say what support exists and what happens if this doesn't work for someone.
6. **Friday.** Say what the meeting is for. Is the decision final, with the details open for input? Or is something still negotiable? Say which. If you call it a discussion when it's settled, people will notice.

## Leave out

- Any suggestion of trust, productivity or "people slacking at home." You don't have grounds for it, and it turns 15 people against the rest of the email.
- The hospital clients' names, unless they're already common knowledge internally.
- Anything that identifies the 4 people who live far away. In a firm of 46, saying "four people more than two hours away" identifies them. That's another reason they should hear it first, in person (see below).
- Words like "culture," "family" or "collaboration" with nothing behind them. Your two concrete facts do the work better.

## Find out first

1. **The dates.** Today is November 16, 2026, so "September 1" means 2027 and "end of next year" means December 31, 2027. Those 4 people would get only four extra months of remote work, then come in one day a week. Is that what you intend? If you meant end of 2028, say so. Either way, put exact dates in the email.
2. **Contracts and employment law.** If "fully remote" is written into the 15 people's contracts or offer letters, changing it may require their consent or a consultation period. Check with an employment lawyer before anything goes out.
3. **Does the 30% figure hold up?** Is the slowdown among remote juniors specifically, or among all juniors hired since 2022? If juniors who come into the office are just as slow, the figure undercuts your case and someone will point it out on Friday. Other things could also explain it: changes in project mix, hiring, or the review criteria. If you can't separate those out, keep the figure but say "part of the reason," not "the reason."
4. **Who was on the two interview teams?** If they were mostly people already in the office, "the team didn't know each other" says more about the partners' one day a week than about remote staff. That's still usable, and it fits your partners point, but you need to know which it is.
5. **What happens to someone who can't do this?** Eleven of the 15 live up to two hours away, so some could face up to four hours of travel on office days. Can you offer travel support, flexible hours on office days, help with relocation, or agreed exit terms? The email has to say something here, because this is the first question on Friday. Only put in what the partners will actually commit to.
6. **Can the office hold all 46 people on the same three days?** Desks, meeting rooms, parking. Finding out it can't in week one would undo the whole message.
7. **What do the other 31 do now?** If they're already in two or three days, this is a small change for them and a big one for 15 people. The email should be pitched to match.

## Second audience

The 15 people hired as fully remote, and especially the 4 who live far away, should not learn this from an all-staff email. A partner should speak to each of them before the email goes out, ideally the same week. Their reaction will shape how everyone else reads the email on Friday.

# Angle and plan for the open letter

## The angle

Make the letter about the plan's members, not the pharmacies' margins. Newspaper readers and the plan's board care whether state employees can still fill a prescription close to home. "We're losing money" is the evidence. "Your members are about to lose their pharmacy" is the story.

The July 1 contract renewal gives you a deadline and a specific decision to point at. Without it you're just complaining. With it you're asking the plan to fix one thing in one contract by one date.

## What it should say, in order

1. **Who's writing and why it matters to the reader.** 38 independent pharmacies serving state employees: teachers, troopers, clerks. Members are about 22% of your prescriptions, so you're a big part of the plan's network, not a small vendor.
2. **The core fact.** Since January the plan pays below cost on the 20 generics you fill most, by $1.80 per prescription on average. State it plainly. This is your strongest and most checkable number.
3. **What's at stake.** In 14 counties the nearest pharmacy for most people is an independent one. If those pharmacies close, members there drive farther or switch to mail order. Name a county or two if you can.
4. **The decision point.** The PBM contract renews July 1. That's when the plan can fix this.
5. **The ask.** One or two specific terms, for example: reimbursement no lower than acquisition cost plus a dispensing fee, and transparency on what the plan pays the PBM compared with what the PBM pays pharmacies. Make it something they can say yes to.
6. **An offer.** Say you'll share your cost data and meet before the contract is finalized. This makes you look reasonable and is hard for them to turn down in public.

## What to leave out or change

- **"A third will close within a year."** Your facts don't support this, and it's the line the plan's spokesperson will go after, which would cost you credibility on everything else. You don't know how many of the 9 closures were caused by rates, and nothing in your list predicts future closures. Either back it up (for example, survey the 38 and report how many say they'd close or leave the network at current rates, stated as exactly that) or drop it. "If nothing changes, pharmacies in these counties will close" is enough without a number.
- **Blaming the 9 closures on rates.** Mention that 9 closed if you want, but don't connect them to rates.
- **Your own 3,100 prescriptions times $1.80.** That multiplication ($5,580) is misleading for two reasons. The $1.80 applies only to your top 20 generics, not all plan prescriptions. And the 3,100 is from last year, before the rate change. If you want a personal example, use this year's numbers for those 20 drugs only.
- **Any joint threat to leave the network.** See antitrust below.

## What to find out first

- **Which July 1?** It's November 2026, so this July has passed. Confirm the renewal is July 1, 2027, and find out when negotiations actually happen. Terms are often set months before the renewal date, so your real deadline may be this winter.
- **Who sets the rates.** Is the low rate the plan's choice, or is the PBM keeping a spread between what the plan pays it and what it pays you? This decides whether you're asking the plan to pay more or to make its PBM pass the money through. That's a very different letter.
- **How you calculated the $1.80.** What did "cost to buy and fill" include, and where did acquisition costs come from (invoices, NADAC)? Expect the plan to say "fill" includes your overhead. Know your answer, and say it's a dispensing cost if it is.
- **The 14 counties.** Which ones, how many plan members live there, and how many of your 38 are in them.
- **Antitrust review.** Publicly petitioning a government body is generally protected. Competitors jointly agreeing on rates, or jointly threatening to drop out, can be risky. Have your state pharmacy association's lawyer, or your own, read the final letter. The association may also already have closure and rate data you can cite.
- **Signatures.** Will all 38 sign by name? Named pharmacies in named towns carry far more weight than "38 pharmacies."
- **Newspaper rules.** Check each paper's word limit (often 600–750 for op-eds, far less for letters) and whether it requires exclusivity. Two papers in the same city often won't both run the same piece. You may need to send the letter to the plan and pitch the papers a news story or separate op-eds.

When you have the July 1 date, the rate-setting answer, and a decision on the one-third claim, the draft will be straightforward.

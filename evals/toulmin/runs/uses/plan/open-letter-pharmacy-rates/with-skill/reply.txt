# Plan for your open letter (no draft yet)

## Who has to be persuaded

The people who decide are whoever approves the plan's pharmacy benefit manager (PBM) contract before July 1. Find out who that is: a board of trustees, a state agency head, or a legislative committee. Write the letter to them by name. The newspapers are how you put pressure on them, and newspaper readers include state employees and legislators. Two groups are likely second audiences, each needing a different argument later: **plan members in the 14 counties**, who are the people the plan has to answer to, and **legislators** who oversee the plan.

## The angle

**Don't make this about pharmacies losing money. Make it about the plan's own members losing their pharmacy.** The plan doesn't owe you a margin. It does owe its members access to care. The reasoning the decision-makers will accept is: *a plan that pays below cost for its members' most common prescriptions will lose the pharmacies that fill them, and in 14 counties those pharmacies are the nearest ones its members have.* Your facts support that.

**The ask is tied to July 1:** when the plan renews its PBM contract, it should require that pharmacies are paid at least what the drug costs plus a dispensing fee. Pick one specific, checkable standard. A common one is a published benchmark such as NADAC (Medicaid's survey of what pharmacies pay for drugs) plus a set fee. A date and a specific contract term are much harder to dismiss than a general complaint.

## Order on the page

1. **Who's writing and the ask.** 38 independent pharmacies, the July 1 renewal, the one contract term you want.
2. **The core fact.** Since January, the plan pays about $1.80 less than cost on each prescription for the 20 generics you fill most.
3. **Why it matters to the plan.** Members are about 22% of your prescriptions, so this isn't a side line you can absorb. In 14 counties an independent is the nearest pharmacy for most people.
4. **One concrete pharmacy.** Yours: about 3,100 member prescriptions last year, what the loss comes to for you, and what you'd have to cut.
5. **Closures, stated honestly.** 9 independents closed in two years. Say you can't attribute them all to rates. Saying so earns trust.
6. **Answer the two objections the plan will raise** (below).
7. **What happens if they say no**, and an offer: meet before renewal, and let them check your invoices.

## Drop "a third will close within a year"

That claim is a prediction, and your facts don't support it. Nine closures in two years is about 4–5 a year, and you don't know why they closed. I don't know how many independents the state has, but if it's around 120, a third would be 40 closures in one year, roughly ten times the recent pace. A reporter will check the number. The plan's spokesperson will call it scaremongering, and the rest of the letter will lose credibility with it.

**A better version you can stand behind:** survey the 38 signers. Ask each one whether, if the contract renews on the same terms, they would (a) stop taking plan members, (b) cut hours or services, or (c) close. "11 of the 38 pharmacies signing this letter say they will leave the plan's network or close if the terms don't change" is a fact nobody can call speculation. It's also more alarming, because it names a number and a date.

## Find out before writing

1. **Who decides, and their timeline.** When does negotiation actually close? It's probably well before July 1.
2. **What the $1.80 covers.** It applies to your top 20 generics only, so you **can't** multiply it by 3,100. You need the share of your plan prescriptions that are those 20 drugs, and your net margin across *all* plan prescriptions. The plan will say you make money on brand-name drugs, so know the answer first.
3. **What it was before January.** If the rates went from break-even to −$1.80 in one renewal cycle, that's your strongest ground. It shows a change someone made, and someone can undo it.
4. **What the plan pays the PBM** for the same drugs. This is a state plan, so a public records request may get it. If the plan pays the PBM more than the PBM pays you, the fix might cost the plan nothing. That answers the "this will raise premiums" objection. Only say it if you have documents.
5. **Geography.** For the 14 counties, how many plan members live there, and how far is the next pharmacy if the independent closes? "A 40-minute drive" will matter more to readers than "14 counties."
6. **The total number of independents in the state.** That gives context for the 9.

## Objections to answer in the letter

- **"The PBM sets the rates, not us."** Answer: the plan sets the PBM's contract terms, and July 1 is when it can.
- **"Paying more raises costs for taxpayers."** Answer it with what the fix costs, worked out from your top-20 volumes, set against what members lose. Use the PBM-spread figure if you get it.

## Leave out or handle carefully

- **Anything that sounds like a coordinated boycott.** Thirty-eight competitors jointly saying "we'll all leave the network" can raise antitrust problems. The FTC has pursued pharmacies over collective action. Describe each pharmacy's own situation, and have a lawyer or your state pharmacy association read the letter before it goes to the papers.
- **Claims about the PBM's conduct you can't document.** It's going in newspapers, so stick to your own numbers.
- **Blaming the 9 closures on rates.**
- **Your general frustration.** One page goes fast. Every line should be about members, money or July 1.

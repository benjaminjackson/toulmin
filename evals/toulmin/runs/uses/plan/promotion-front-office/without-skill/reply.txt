## The angle

You've already been doing the job for seven weeks, and the front desk team is better because of changes you made. That's what the page should argue. Don't build it on the guest score.

## Don't claim you're the main reason scores went up

Your GM approved and lived through the renovation. If the page says you're the main reason check-in scores rose during an 18-month lobby and room renovation, the GM will notice the renovation first and may start doubting your other numbers. That claim would be the weakest line on the page and would weaken everything around it.

You can still use the score. Present it as one result among several: "Check-in scores rose from 7.9 to 8.6 over the same period I rebuilt training and cut turnover." That's true, and the GM can draw the connection. Go further only if you find evidence that separates your work from the renovation (see below).

## Order

1. **The ask, one sentence.** You're applying for front office manager, with six years at this hotel and two as assistant.
2. **The last seven weeks.** This is your strongest evidence because it's the real job, done alone. Be specific: what you ran, what problems you handled, what didn't slip (scores, staffing, complaints, audits). Leading with this answers "can you do it?" before the GM asks.
3. **What you built.** The training rewrite (desk-ready in 5 shifts instead of 9) and turnover falling from 64% to 36%. Present these as management results, not just process changes.
4. **Guest results.** The score, framed carefully as above.
5. **The budget gap, stated plainly, with a plan.** The GM already knows you haven't managed a budget. If you leave it out, it looks like you're hoping they won't notice. If you raise it yourself, it reads as self-awareness. Pair it with something concrete, like asking to sit with the controller or GM on the payroll budget in your first 90 days, or taking a specific course. Show that you already think about labor cost (next section).
6. **Close.** One or two lines on what you'd focus on first in the role.

## Find out before writing

- **Turnover and training savings in dollars.** This is the most useful item on the list because it connects your work to the $1.1M payroll budget you'd own. Estimate the cost per hire (recruiting, paid training shifts, overtime to cover gaps), multiply it by the 4 fewer departures, and add 4 saved training shifts per new hire. Even a rough number shows you think in payroll terms, which answers the budget question before it's asked.
- **Whether the score moved apart from the renovation.** Look at the review site's month-by-month data. Did check-in scores rise before the renovation finished, or in months when rooms were out of service? Do reviews mention the staff, speed, or friendliness, or the new lobby? Did overall and room scores rise as much as check-in? If check-in improved more than the room scores did, that points to your team. If you find this evidence, you can make a stronger claim. If you don't, keep the framing modest.
- **What happened during the seven weeks.** Get the scores, labor hours or overtime against schedule, staffing gaps you filled, and any problems you resolved. Any labor or payroll decisions you made in that period count as budget experience.
- **The turnover comparison.** Was there anything else behind the drop, like a wage increase, a change in the local job market, or a new scheduling policy? If there was, mention it briefly or choose your words carefully. The GM may know.
- **The job description, or what the GM cares about.** Write to the GM's priorities, not a generic list of qualifications.

## Leave out

- The "main reason" claim, unless the data supports it.
- Years of service as a selling point. Mention six years once, in the opening line. Tenure isn't a qualification for the job.
- Anything about the previous manager.
- Apologetic language about the budget. State the gap, give the plan, and move on.

If you can only do one piece of homework before Friday, do the turnover and training cost estimate. It turns your best results into something that speaks to the budget, your biggest gap.

## The angle

**Pitch it as a finished, heavily tested game with a costed plan to deliver it. Don't call it a "proven hit."**

A hit means sales, and the game hasn't sold yet. The 8.1 average comes from 23 ratings. Experienced backers know that a small pre-release score on that site often comes partly from playtesters and friends. They'll see "proven hit" next to "first-time designer" and start doubting everything else on the page.

Your real evidence is stronger than the label. **140 sessions with about 60 people over three years** is an unusual amount of testing, and few first campaigns can show it. You can state it flatly and nobody can argue with it.

## The argument in brief

- **Claim:** This game is finished and well tested, and backing it is a safe bet that it will reach you.
- **Grounds:** 140 sessions, about 60 testers, 2 conventions, 8.1 from 23 raters, a factory quote in hand, and 1,240 people waiting for launch.
- **The link the reader needs to accept:** A game that has been played and revised this many times, with real costs behind it, is likely to be good and likely to ship.
- **Main objection:** "First-time creator. Will this ever arrive, and is the money enough?"
- **Wording:** Make firm claims about testing. Make careful claims about popularity.

## What the numbers say (check this before writing)

- **$28,000 ÷ $49 ≈ 572 backers.** That's 46% of your email list. Hitting it on the list alone would need a high conversion rate, so you'll also need backers from the platform itself.
- **Cost at goal:** $17,400 factory + 572 × $9 shipping ≈ **$22,550.** That leaves about $5,450. Platform and payment fees usually take around 8–10%, roughly $2,500 (check your platform's actual rates). That leaves about **$2,900** for everything else: tariffs, freight from the factory, packaging, damaged copies, art and editing if those aren't paid for yet.
- **1,500 copies against 572 backers** leaves about 930 spare copies. Backers will ask what they're for. If they're stock for retail or later sales, that's a good thing to say.

The budget is tight but it works. Showing it openly is your best answer to "will it ship," and it fits that you're a land surveyor. You measure things for a living, and you can say so in one line without overplaying it.

## Suggested order

1. **The game:** the family, the winter, the lighthouse, and what you actually do at the table. Co-op, 1–4 players.
2. **What backers get and the price:** $49 with US shipping, plus a delivery date.
3. **Why you can trust it's good:** the testing record first, then conventions, then the rating, stated plainly as 23 raters.
4. **Who made it:** first game, day job, three years of work. Say it directly. Hiding it reads worse.
5. **Where the money goes:** the factory quote, shipping, fees.
6. **Risks and how you're handling them.**
7. **The ask.**

## Leave out

- "Proven hit," "bestseller," or anything that implies sales.
- The rating as a headline. Put it in the evidence section with the number of raters.

## Find out first (most important first)

1. **Is the game actually finished?** Final art, edited rules, a production sample in hand? This is the main proof that it will ship, and it's missing from your facts.
2. **What's in the $17,400 quote, and what's left out?** Tariffs, freight to a fulfillment center, international shipping (you've only priced the US), art you still owe.
3. **A delivery date you can stand behind,** and what you'll do if it slips.
4. **Who gave the 23 ratings?** If most weren't playtesters or friends, say so. That makes the 8.1 much stronger.
5. **Anything quotable from conventions or testers,** or any previews or reviews lined up. Specific reactions persuade better than a score.
6. **What sets the game apart:** play time, ages, and the one mechanic that makes it feel like getting a family through a winter. That decides your opening paragraph.

Once you've answered these, the 500 words will mostly be choosing what goes in.

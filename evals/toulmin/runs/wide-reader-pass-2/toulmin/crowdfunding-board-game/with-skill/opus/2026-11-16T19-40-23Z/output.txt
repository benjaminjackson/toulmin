## The plan

- **Who decides:** board gamers who back crowdfunding projects. Many have been burned before by first-time creators, and many will look the game up on the ratings site before they pledge.
- **Claim:** this game has been tested hard, the people who play it rate it highly, and the campaign has a costed plan for delivering it.
- **Grounds:** 140 playtest sessions with about 60 people since 2023. Two conventions. An 8.1 average from 23 ratings. 1,240 email signups. A factory quote of $17,400 for 1,500 copies. Shipping at about $9 a copy.
- **Warrant:** a game that has been played many times by many people, and still rates well, is likely to be good. A creator who has costed the whole job before asking for money is likely to deliver.
- **Rebuttals a backer will raise:** "First-time creator, so will I ever get it?" The pitch answers this with the factory quote and the budget. "Only 23 ratings, probably friends." The pitch is open about how many ratings there are.
- **Qualifier:** "proven at the table," not "proven hit."

## "Proven hit": what your facts can support

- **What the claim says:** a hit is a game that has sold well and been widely played. The game hasn't been sold yet, and 23 ratings is a small sample.
- **What the facts support:** the game is proven at the table. It has been through 140 sessions with about 60 people, and the people who rated it gave it an 8.1. I wrote the pitch on that basis because I couldn't check with you. This is my recommendation, but it's your decision.
- **What a skeptical backer would say to "proven hit,"** which your facts can't answer:
  - "It hasn't sold a single copy."
  - "23 ratings is tiny, and ratings from playtesters and convention visitors tend to run high."

  Backers can check that rating count in seconds. If they find it after reading "proven hit," they'll start doubting the true parts of your pitch too.

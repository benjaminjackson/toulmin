I've left out the line about every other publisher paying at least $12,000, because your own facts don't support it. You have one publisher at $12,500. Your agent's range starts at $11,000, which is below $12,000. Editors talk to agents and other editors, so a claim like that is easy to check. If they find it's wrong, it could cost you more than the raise is worth.

The true version is still strong: you were paid $12,500 last year for the same kind of book, and you're asking for less than that. That makes $11,500 look reasonable.

<final_document>
Subject: Fee for [Title]

Hi [Editor's name],

Thank you for offering me [Title]. I've loved making six books with [Publisher], and I'd very much like to do this one.

Before we finalize, I'd like to talk about the fee. My fee has been $9,000 per book since our first book together four years ago. Since then, two of our books have sold over 20,000 copies each, and [Book] made the [State] reading list. I think that record has earned a step up.

Each book takes me about four months, so $9,000 comes to about $2,250 a month. Last year another publisher paid me $12,500 for a picture book of the same length. My agent tells me $11,000 to $13,000 is the usual range for an illustrator with my sales.

For this book, I'm asking for $11,500. That's still less than I was paid elsewhere last year, and it would let me keep making room in my schedule for your books.

I'm happy to talk it over by phone if that's easier. I'm looking forward to getting started.

Best,
[Your name]
</final_document>

The email is about 190 words.

- **"Keep making room in my schedule"** gently tells them what happens if they say no, without making a threat. Take it out if it feels too pointed for your relationship with this editor.
- **Your agent:** if your agent normally handles your fees, check whether they'd rather send this themselves or be copied in.

I drafted this letter for the people who decide on admissions. It makes one argument: someone who has handled real responsibility on his own and learned a technical tool on the job is likely to do well in the program and get a lot out of it.

Dear Admissions Committee,

I am the manager of [Farm Name], a 400-cow dairy operation. I am writing to recommend Tomás [Last Name] for your two-year Agricultural Technology program. Tomás has worked for us for three years, since he was 19. He is one of the most dependable people I have employed, and he has already shown the curiosity about the technical side of dairy that your program is built to develop.

Tomás runs our 3 a.m. milking shift by himself, five days a week. Nobody else is on the farm at that hour. If something goes wrong with a cow or the equipment, he decides what to do. I give that shift to someone only when I'm confident it will be done right without anyone checking, and Tomás has earned that confidence. In three years he has missed two shifts.

What makes me sure he belongs in your program is how he has taken to the data side of the work. He learned our herd management software and now uses it to flag sick cows for our veterinarian. Last winter he caught nine cases of mastitis early. In a herd our size, early detection is the difference between a cow that recovers quickly and one we lose production from or lose altogether. Nobody required this of him. He saw that the software could tell him something and he learned to read it. I believe formal training will multiply what he is already doing on his own.

In the interest of a complete picture: Tomás's spoken English is strong, and his written English is still developing. He knows this, and he is taking a night class to improve it on top of his full-time work. That is typical of how he handles a weakness. He names it and works on it.

Tomás has the work habits, judgment, and practical experience to succeed in your program, and the dairy industry needs people like him with formal technical training. I recommend him without reservation. Please contact me at [phone] or [email] if I can tell you more.

Sincerely,

[Your Name]
Farm Manager, [Farm Name]

Some choices you may want to change:

- **The 5-week absence isn't in the letter.** Admissions won't know about it and wouldn't ask. A family emergency in the middle of a long, steady record doesn't count against his ability to finish a program. If you'd rather say "two missed shifts" only with the absence acknowledged, change it to "Outside of a family medical emergency, he has missed two shifts in three years." That's accurate and costs him nothing.
- **I did mention the written English.** His application essays will probably show it anyway. If you raise it first and point to the night class, the committee sees he's working on it, and the rest of your praise becomes more believable. If you think it hurts him, cut that paragraph and the letter still works.
- **Every number is one you gave me.** I didn't add savings figures or vet outcomes for the mastitis cases. If you know roughly what those nine early catches saved in treatment costs or lost milk, one sentence with that figure would be the strongest line in the letter.

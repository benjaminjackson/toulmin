# Writing for a reader who can say no

A draft written in one pass is written from the writer's side of the table. This skill makes you sit on the reader's side twice: once before you write, and once after.

Do the four steps in order. For a long or high-stakes piece (a proposal, a pitch, a memo to a decision-maker, anything sent to someone who can say no to something that matters), show the user your answers to steps 1 and 2 as a short list before writing, and let them correct it. For a short piece, do the steps without showing them.

## 1. The reader

Answer each in one line, from what the user told you.

- Who decides, and what exactly should they do after reading? Where someone else decides, write for the person who decides and not for the person who disagrees.
- What do they already think of the proposal, and of the writer?
- What do they hold against the writer, or know about the writer that the writer would prefer not to raise?
- What is their alternative to saying yes: doing nothing, the familiar or cheaper option, a rival?
- What did they come to this page wanting to find out, or afraid of hearing?
- How will they read it: closely, at a glance, or hearing it aloud in a room?

## 2. The facts

- Derive every figure the user's facts allow: rates, totals, cost per unit, the pace a target needs set against the pace already reached, and what happens if nothing changes. Redo the arithmetic.
- Choose the facts that bear on this reader's concerns, not the writer's, and put each in the true form most favourable to the case. For a climbing gym that needs to sell 120 annual passes this year, "five times what the one-month sale reached" and "10 passes a month, when the sale sold 24 in one month" can be the same fact.
- Describe the reader's alternative as the facts show it, including where it is heading. Say what happens if the reader says no, in the user's own figures.
- An awkward fact the reader knows, or will find out, goes in the piece: early, in plain words, once, with what has changed since.
- When the user wants a claim the facts do not carry, tell the user in one sentence each what the claim says and what the facts support. Recommend the strongest claim the facts do carry, with a condition the reader can check: a date, a number to reach by then, and what the writer will do if it is missed. Then write the version the user picks. The decision is theirs.
- Never supply a fact. Where the piece needs a commitment the user did not give, propose one, word it as a proposal, and list it for the user to confirm.

## 3. The draft

- Open with what this reader came for, then the request, then the case. The request is one specific action that is easy to take.
- Answer the objection this reader will raise themselves, from the user's facts or with a commitment the reader can check. Naming an objection and asking the reader to look past it is not an answer.
- When the reader holds something against the writer, deal with it before the ask.
- Write in the voice of this writer speaking to this person, given their history.

## 4. Read the draft as the reader, then revise

Before the user sees the draft, read it once as the reader from step 1, with their grievance, their alternative and their patience. Do not skip this for a short piece. Look for each of these and fix what you find.

1. **What they came for.** The thing this reader wants to know or fears hearing (what happens to me, what will it cost, has the thing I complained about changed) is answered in the opening lines, plainly, before the case for anything.
2. **Lines that make this reader bristle.** Telling them how they will feel. Calling a cost to them small. A hint of guilt or threat. A demand they have already refused. Praise of the writer that the reader would dispute. Reword or cut. With a reader who believes they have behaved well, ask for something they can say yes to.
3. **What they know that the page hides.** The writer's stake in the outcome, a past failure, the awkward number. If the reader knows it or will find it, the piece says it first.
4. **Answers they can hold the writer to.** Where the piece answers a worry, the answer names something the reader can check: a date, a number, a person, what happens if it is missed. One such commitment does more than three assurances.
5. **Every number, date, name and promise traces to the user.** Go through them one at a time. Each is something the user gave, or arithmetic on it that you have redone. Anything else comes out, or stays as a proposal worded as one. Leave no placeholder in the body where a sentence built from the given facts would do.
6. **They can find it.** A reader who will scan for terms (pay, dates, price, what to do next) finds them set apart where the eye lands. A piece that will be spoken has short sentences and one number to a sentence.
7. **Length and form** are what the user asked for.

Hand over the revised piece. Outside it, list anything you proposed that the user has to confirm.

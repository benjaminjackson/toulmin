# Toulmin, Rieke and Janik, *An Introduction to Reasoning*

Source: Stephen Toulmin, Richard Rieke and Allan Janik, *An Introduction to Reasoning*, second edition, Macmillan, 1984. Page and chapter numbers are the book's own. The text was read from a scan, so a page number can be off by one.

## Building blocks

### Play the questioner (ch. 2)

The book presents every argument as an exchange between a person who asserts and a person who questions. Each part of the argument is the answer to something the questioner asks. Draft an argument by playing the questioner against your own claim and writing down the answer at each step.

### Claim (ch. 3)

Making good on a claim comes down to three jobs:

1. Point to the accepted, relevant facts the claim rests on.
2. Say what general rule or principle makes those facts relevant.
3. Show that the facts support *this* claim and not a rival one.

**Settle an unclear claim before anything else.** If the wording can be read more than one way, each reading needs different grounds and leads to a different action. "My brother is mad" could mean angry, legally incompetent, or psychotic. The questioner's first move is "Just what are you saying?"

### Grounds (chs. 4 and 23)

What can serve as grounds: observations, matters of common knowledge, statistics, personal testimony, and claims established earlier.

- **The claim can be no stronger than the grounds.**
- **Grounds have to be accepted by the other side.** What you offer as fact will not always strike the reader as fact.
- **Grounds have to be checkable without taking your word for it.** "It is completely false" gives the reader nothing. Dates, the parties involved and a public record give them something to check (pp. 241 to 243).

Nine questions for testing a set of grounds (p. 243):

1. Is there enough information?
2. Is it clear?
3. Can it be verified by someone other than the person offering it?
4. Is it consistent with other grounds?
5. Is it consistent with itself?
6. Is the source competent?
7. Is the source biased?
8. If there are statistics, are they sound?
9. Is the information current, and the best available?

### Warrant (chs. 5, 12 and 22)

**The warrant is what makes grounds relevant.** Relevance cannot be judged from the facts and the claim alone (pp. 115 to 116).

**The same grounds can support different claims under different warrants.** The book builds three claims from one set of facts: one by a causal warrant, which is hard to defend, one by reading the facts as signs, which is more likely to survive, and one by analogy. The choice depends on how strong a claim is wanted and on the audience (pp. 214 to 216).

### Backing (ch. 6)

A warrant can be challenged in two directions:

1. Is that warrant reliable at all?
2. Does it really apply to this specific case?

Writers skip the second. A general rule can be sound and still not fit the case in front of you.

### Chains of arguments (ch. 7)

A claim can serve as the grounds of the next argument. Test the link from grounds to claim, then go behind the grounds: treat each ground as a claim and look at what it rests on (pp. 74 to 75). Going backward shows the hidden assumptions. Going forward shows the consequences. The book gives no rule for how far down to go. A workable one: stop at grounds this reader will accept without argument.

## Who has to prove what (ch. 11)

**An argument needs an occasion.** The test question is "Why does this particular position need to be justified?" A challenger who cannot answer it may be brushed aside (pp. 103 to 104).

**The challenger goes first.** The person who questions an accepted position has to "show cause" with a genuine, specific ground for doubt. Only then does the other side have to justify the position. A food preservative in long use is presumed harmless. Once real evidence of risk appears, the burden moves to the food processors (pp. 104 to 107).

**A presumption** is an opinion it is reasonable to hold until a strong enough argument is made against it. Every field has its own. In science it is the established body of knowledge. In law the defendant is presumed not liable (p. 108). In ethics, whoever departs from the usual understanding carries the burden (pp. 401 to 402).

For a writer this decides how much work the piece has to do. If you argue against what the reader presumes, the burden is yours, and the piece must first give them a specific reason to doubt. If you argue for what the reader presumes, the job is mostly to answer the challenge.

**A reasonable conclusion is also a timely one.** Aim for beyond reasonable doubt in the time available, and not for certainty (pp. 108 to 109).

**A quandary** is a question with no clear way to settle it: two incompatible claims are both strongly supported, or neither is (p. 110). Refusing both helps no one when something real is at stake. The way out is a presumption together with a standard of proof. A jury does not weigh two stories against each other. It asks whether the prosecution proved its case beyond reasonable doubt (p. 111). When the arguments cannot settle a matter, say which way the presumption runs and what it would take to overturn it.

## What the setting needs (chs. 12 and 23)

An argument is strong because it serves the purpose of the activity it belongs to, and is put to the right audience in the right forum (pp. 113 to 115).

**Whose agreement is needed** (pp. 254 to 255):

- **Science and criticism aim at consensus.** The goal is to bring any informed questioner to the same view. In criticism partial agreement is enough: "Yes, I see what you mean."
- **A court uses an adversary procedure.** The side that brings the case must meet the standard of proof and persuade the judge or jury. Whether the other side is convinced does not matter.
- **Business and public policy need a practical decision.** Nobody expects everyone to agree. The work is to weigh likely gains against possible losses.

In an adversary setting, write for the person who decides and not for the opponent.

**What a claim asks of the reader** (p. 254). A prediction asks "What are we to expect?" Criticism asks "What attitude can one reasonably take?" A legal plea asks "Who is to be upheld?" A business proposal asks "What are we to decide?"

**Settle the standpoint before arguing.** An executive and a singer discuss the sale of a concert hall. One argues finance, the other culture, and each thinks the other ignorant (pp. 239 to 240).

**"Context determines criteria"** (p. 256). A medical diagnosis and a court ruling both need a strong argument, each of its own kind. Compare arguments only with others of the same type.

## Kinds of argument and how to test each (ch. 22)

Sorted by the kind of link between grounds and claim. The list is a set of samples, and the kinds run into each other.

### Analogy (pp. 216 to 219)

Two things share enough features that what is true of one is taken to be true of the other.

Tests: Are the points of likeness many? Do they bear directly on the claim? Are there relevant differences? A critic looks for the one difference that destroys the comparison.

A figurative comparison, such as a pension fund and a well running dry, can make a point clear. It cannot be the warrant for a claim.

### Generalisation (pp. 219 to 222)

What holds in a sample is taken to hold in the whole population.

Tests: Is the sample representative? Is it large enough that more cases would not change the result? Was it chosen objectively and measured accurately? Is it recent enough? Are there cases that go the other way?

### Sign (pp. 222 to 225)

The sign and the thing it points to are taken to occur together reliably. This fits subjects too complicated for exact argument: economic indicators, diplomacy, circumstantial evidence, symptoms.

Test: How certainly is the sign tied to what it signals? An innocent person afraid of being accused can behave just like a guilty one. An opponent can answer one list of signs with another list.

### Cause (pp. 226 to 229)

Stronger than sign. The events are claimed to be causally connected and not only found together.

The book's three questions: What is the reasoning for the causal link? Does the argument meet the tests for a generalisation? What rebuttals are possible?

A critic also asks: Were other causes overlooked? Is one cause too simple an account? Could the cause run both ways? Could a third thing be driving both?

Sound support for a causal claim: the one circumstance all the cases share, the one factor that differs between otherwise like cases, a control group, or two things that rise and fall together with third factors ruled out.

### Authority (pp. 230 to 232)

Using an authority is not the fault. Failing to establish the authority is.

Eight conditions (p. 232):

1. The authority had recent and full access to the facts.
2. The authority is named, and is not "a well-known commentator."
3. Their education, training and experience are shown.
4. Other qualified people in the field acknowledge them.
5. They have a record that deserves respect.
6. Their credentials are current.
7. The judgment is within their own field.
8. The judgment has been weighed against that of other qualified experts.

### Dilemma (pp. 233 to 234)

Only two explanations or choices are said to exist, and both are bad. Tests: Has the dilemma been established for this specific case? Is there a third possibility that fits the facts? A dilemma that depends on its wording can be answered by challenging the terms.

## How arguments go wrong (chs. 14 to 20)

A fallacy is an argument that can seem persuasive although it is unsound.

- **No form of argument is fallacious in itself.** The same argument can be a fallacy in one setting and solid in another (pp. 131, 177 to 178).
- **The list is never complete,** and the five groups below are a convenience. The aim is a feel for where arguments go wrong and not skill at naming faults.
- **When you suspect a fault, ask about the warrant and backing.** State the warrant and the weakness usually shows. Unsound arguments mislead by leaving things out (pp. 133, 154).

### Five questions to check any argument (p. 179)

Each matches one group below.

1. Are grounds really being offered?
2. Are they directly relevant to the substance of the claim?
3. Is there enough evidence?
4. Are the presumptions justifiable?
5. Is anything ambiguous?

### How to repair a fault (pp. 178 to 179)

A fault is not fatal. The arguer restates and does not have to withdraw.

1. **Change the claim.** Make it weaker or more exact.
2. **Change the grounds.** Add more, or find ones that bear more directly.
3. **State the warrant.** Steps 1 and 2 change what the warrant has to cover.

One case offered for a general claim fails as stated. The same case is fine in support of a claim that begins "it is possible that."

### 1. Missing grounds (ch. 15)

The argument looks as if it offers evidence and offers none.

- **Begging the question.** The "grounds" say the same thing as the claim in other words: Smith is telling the truth because he would not lie to me. Test: state the warrant and ask how it would be backed. The backing turns out to need the claim itself.
- **Question-begging epithets.** A loaded phrase such as "cowardly pacifist" slips in a judgment as if it were description. Test: pull the phrase apart. The words "obvious" and "evident" do similar work.

### 2. Irrelevant grounds (ch. 16)

The evidence is real but is the wrong kind for this claim.

- **Evading the issue.** The grounds miss the point in dispute: teachers are valued, so they deserve a 10 per cent rise. A **red herring** takes the discussion down another track. A **straw man** restates the other side's position in a distorted form that is easy to knock down.
- **Appeal to authority.** A fault when the authority is treated as the last word and used to stop inquiry, or speaks outside their field. Fix: say who the authority is and why they count on this question.
- **Argument against the person.** Rejecting a claim because of something damaging about the person who makes it: name-calling, guilt by association, or treating a whole group as unable to be objective. Bias has to be shown about the individual.
- **Argument from ignorance.** A claim is held to be true because its opposite has not been proved.
- **Appeal to the people.** Justifying a claim by its popularity or by group identity. An appeal to tradition is a fault only when it is used to stop discussion.
- **Appeal to compassion.** A moving story that hides the question at issue. Not always a fault: sometimes the feeling is relevant.
- **Appeal to force.** A threat gets compliance and not agreement. A sound argument from self-interest is different: "without a pension you risk poverty" has real backing.

### 3. Defective grounds (ch. 17)

The evidence is the right kind and there is too little of it.

- **Hasty generalisation.** A general conclusion from too few cases, or from cases that are not typical.
- **Accident.** A general rule is applied without checking whether this case is one of its exceptions. The warning sign is a rule stated as absolute: "nobody is ever justified in..."

### 4. Unwarranted assumptions (ch. 18)

The argument assumes a warrant is widely shared when it is not.

- **Complex question.** Two questions are put as one, so any answer concedes something: "Have you stopped abusing drugs?" Fix: split it and check what each part assumes.
- **False cause.** "After" is taken for "because," or a bare correlation is taken for a cause. A causal claim needs a general mechanism behind it. Fix: state the warrant, check its backing, and use a qualifier.
- **False analogy.** Two things alike in some respects are assumed to be alike in the respect that matters. Test: look for a relevant difference. Any further likeness "must be established independently" (p. 163).
- **Poisoning the wells.** A claim is made immune to evidence. The arguer refuses to qualify the warrant when the evidence calls for it, and reads every counterexample as confirmation or as bad faith.

### 5. Ambiguities (ch. 19)

The trouble is in the meaning of the words.

- **Equivocation.** One word is used in two senses within one argument.
- **Amphiboly.** Grammar, punctuation or word order allows two readings. Fix: rewrite the sentence.
- **Accent.** Emphasis misleads: a quotation taken out of context, or large type that makes a literally true statement give a false impression. Fix: read the draft as the reader will, and tone down the headline.
- **Composition and division.** What is true of every part is claimed of the whole, or the reverse. Sodium and chlorine are poisons, so salt must be.
- **Figure of speech.** Similar form is read as similar meaning, or every noun is assumed to name a thing.

### Two more faults, not in the book

From Andrew Pineau, "The Abuses of Argument" (*Informal Logic*, 2013).

- **A ground stated as fact that the reader will not accept without support.** A good warrant does not save it.
- **Hiding or ignoring a known rebuttal,** which makes the warrant look stronger than it is.

## Wording (ch. 21)

- **Whoever controls the definitions can win almost any argument** (pp. 210 to 211). If the other side's case depends on how a word is defined, challenge the definition. Do not argue inside it.
- **Intense language can lower the writer's credibility** (p. 206). A figure of speech that fits can raise it.
- **Readers read charitably** (pp. 208 to 209). They assume the writer has a point and may supply a better ground than the writer intended.

## Arguing in business and other organisations (ch. 29)

**Claims** are mostly proposals for action toward a goal that is already agreed. They rest on projections, and the projections must be specific because they will soon be checked against results (pp. 373 to 374). Failing to decide is itself a decision.

**The budget is the real qualifier** (pp. 374, 388). Every proposal means less for something else, and agreement that comes with no money attached means nothing.

**Grounds** carry the most weight in this field (pp. 383 to 385). They are factual and usually numbers: the organisation's own reports, outside data on customers, regulation, prices and competitors, and estimates of risk given as chances. One ground is special to this field: the list of every workable alternative, each with its estimated outcome and risk. A proposal that shows only the favoured option has left a ground out.

Three cautions about numbers (pp. 376 to 377):

- Estimates get confused with targets.
- Six estimates that are each 85 per cent likely can combine to a result that is less than 50 per cent likely.
- Once a number goes into a model it loses the qualifications it came with.

**Warrants** (pp. 385 to 388). The base warrant in a business is: do what promises profit without endangering the company. Others: what has worked should continue, what worked for another company will work for us, cut costs, raise efficiency. They are usually left unstated because everyone in the organisation shares its goals.

The trouble comes when a different warrant turns up. One ground (fuel reserves are limited) under two warrants gives two opposite claims. An argument that always worked inside the company stops working on an audience that argues from an environmental warrant.

**Nonprofits and government cannot rest on a business's warrants.** Service may outweigh income (p. 388). An argument to a nonprofit's board, or to a donor, has to state the warrant that a commercial audience would take for granted.

**Backing** is the organisation's definition of itself: its objectives, its values and its place in society (p. 388).

**Rebuttals come from other people** (p. 389). Each decision involves several people, some of them chosen because they find flaws. A proposal should answer their objections before it is put forward.

## Arguing about right and wrong (ch. 30)

**The fight is over the description.** Familiar principles, such as keep promises, do not deceive and be fair, are seldom disputed. What is disputed is whether one of them applies to this case (p. 408). Whether a prisoner's false statements are called "lying" or "loyalty" settles the verdict "nine tenths of the way" (p. 399). Most of the work goes into establishing how the facts should be described.

A person defending an action has two moves (p. 409). They can accept the label and plead an exception ("a white lie"), or argue that the word does not properly apply.

**Cite only the facts relevant to the principle you invoke** (p. 407).

**Qualifiers and rebuttals matter more here than warrant and backing** (pp. 400, 412 to 413). Few ethical rules are absolute, so claims are worded "presumably" and carry their exceptions: lying, so presumably wrong, unless done out of loyalty. When two principles conflict, either one can be the warrant and the other the rebuttal. The aim is to respect the spirit of both.

**Backing** is usually left unstated. When given, it is general agreement, consequences, or a community's way of life (p. 410).

## Law, science and the arts (chs. 26 to 28)

- **Law.** Good grounds are evidence that survives cross-examination. On appeal the warrant is precedent: like facts get a like decision. Rebuttal is built into the procedure through opposing counsel.
- **Science.** Grounds are reports of fact. Warrants are laws, formulas and models, backed by past experience that they work and by their fit with the wider scientific picture.
- **The arts.** An interpretation is a circumstantial case built from details of the work. Qualifiers often name a standpoint: "as a matter of plot construction." Such an argument convinces by being "rich, complex, and true to life" and not by being rigorous (p. 360). It can end with the other person seeing what you see, short of agreeing.

## How to argue fairly (ch. 24)

Listen. See the issue as your adversary sees it. Accept the verdict of the forum you agreed to (p. 266).

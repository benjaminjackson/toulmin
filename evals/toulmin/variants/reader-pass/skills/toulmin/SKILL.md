# Toulmin's model of argument

A way to build an argument that a reader can follow from what they already accept to what you want them to accept, and a way to find where an existing argument breaks.

Read a reference file before relying on anything that is not on this page.

| File | Read it when |
|---|---|
| `references/karbach-paper.md` | You want to see the planning questions and the checking questions answered for one real argument, or an example of when to state the warrant. |
| `references/uses-of-argument.md` | You cannot tell which sentence is the warrant, the grounds or the backing. Or you have to decide how strongly to word a claim, how to admit or dismiss a rival view, or how to judge a claim that turned out wrong. |
| `references/toulmin-book.md` | You are checking an argument and need the named ways arguments go wrong and how to repair each. Or the argument rests on an analogy, a generalisation, a sign, a cause, an authority or a dilemma, and you need the tests for that kind. Or you need to know who carries the burden of proof, or what arguing looks like in a business, a nonprofit, an ethical dispute, a court, science or criticism. |

## The six parts

Every argument has the first three, stated or not. Add the other three when the argument needs them.

| Part | What it is | The question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| **Claim** | The position you want the reader to take. | What do I want to prove? |
| **Grounds** | The specific facts the reader will accept as a starting point. | What do I have to go on? |
| **Warrant** | The general statement that makes those facts support that claim. | How do I get from the grounds to the claim? |
| **Backing** | What shows the warrant is reliable, and that it applies to this case. | Why should the reader trust that step? |
| **Rebuttal** | The circumstances in which the claim would not hold. | What could upset the argument? |
| **Qualifier** | The word in the claim that says how sure you are: "certainly", "presumably", "probably", "may". | How strongly can I state this? |

Read aloud, the parts join up like this: *[grounds]. Since [warrant], because [backing], so [qualifier] [claim], unless [rebuttal].* If that sentence does not make sense, a part is missing or sits in the wrong slot.

Example: Smoke is pouring from Ann's bedroom. Since smoke is a primary sign of fire, because fires generally produce smoke, so, chances are, Ann's bedroom is on fire, unless the smoke comes from a chemical reaction.

## Three ways to use it

### Planning, before any prose exists

Before the six questions, settle three things about the reader.

- **Who has to be persuaded?** Where someone else decides (a board, a funder, an editor, a judge), write for the person who decides and not for the person who disagrees with you.
- **What does this reader already presume?** The side that argues against the accepted view carries the burden of proof, and has to start by giving a specific reason for doubt. If the reader already leans your way, the piece needs far less.
- **What does the setting need from the argument?** A decision under time pressure, agreement among informed people, or a ruling between two sides. A business or policy reader needs a course of action with gains weighed against losses, set beside the alternatives.

Then answer the six questions in order, in one or two sentences each. Questions 2, 3 and 5 are about the audience and not about you.

1. **What position do I want this audience to take?** That is the claim. State it so it can be read only one way.
2. **Where must this audience begin so that they will take the step I want?** Those are the grounds. Choose facts this reader already accepts or will accept on sight. A fact the reader doubts is not a ground. It is another claim, and it needs its own argument first.
3. **What idea links those grounds to the claim?** That is the warrant. Write it out as one plain general sentence, even if it will never appear in the piece. The same facts support different claims under different warrants, so pick the warrant this reader will grant and see what the facts support under it. If the reader does not hold the warrant at all, more facts will not help: the piece first has to establish the warrant, with cases the reader can check for themselves.
4. **Is the step from grounds to claim safe and reliable?** That is the backing. It has to answer two things: is the warrant sound in general, and does it fit this case?
5. **What could upset the argument?** Those are the rebuttals. Think of what this reader will object to and what would have to be true for the claim to fail.
6. **Is a qualification necessary?** If a rebuttal is live, the claim needs a qualifier, or needs to be narrowed.

Then look at the rebuttals again. Each one that names a person or group who could block the outcome is a second audience, and the argument addressed to them is a different argument with different grounds. In Karbach's example the case to restaurant owners for a service charge turns up the objection "the waitresses may react badly," and that gives the second piece: the case to the waitresses. Tell the user when a plan turns up a second audience.

### Drafting

Work through the six questions first, then write, then read the draft as the reader and revise it (next section). How much of that work the user sees depends on the size of the piece.

- **Long or high-stakes** (a proposal, a pitch, a case for support, an op-ed, a memo to a decision-maker, anything the user will send to someone who can say no to something that matters): show the six parts as a short list before writing any prose. Let the user correct them. Write from the agreed version.
- **Short** (an email of a few paragraphs, a single paragraph, a reply in a thread): work through the six parts without showing them and hand over the prose. Show the breakdown only if the user asks, or if you find a weak spot as described below.

Turning the parts into prose:

- The order of the six questions is the order for thinking. It is not the order for the page. Most persuasive pieces open with the claim or with the ground the reader cares about most.
- **State the warrant when the reader does not already hold it, or when saying it adds force. Leave it unstated when the reader takes it for granted.** A campus administrator knows no more parking can be built, so an argument for a bus service does not need to say so. The director of the bus company does not know that, and without it the facts do not lead anywhere. Spelling out a warrant the reader already holds reads as talking down to them.
- Always know the warrant, even when it stays off the page. An argument whose warrant you cannot write down is one you do not understand yet.
- Use the reader's own concerns as grounds where the facts allow. The restaurant owners are told about staff turnover and profit, not about fairness to waitresses.
- Deal with a rebuttal in the prose when the reader is likely to raise it themselves, and answer it: from the user's facts, or with a step the writer proposes, worded as a proposal. Naming an objection and then asking the reader to look past it, or leaving it for someone else to solve, is not an answer. Do not state as fact a reassuring detail the user never gave. When the reader already holds something against the writer, deal with it before the ask. Leave a rebuttal out when the reader is unlikely to raise it, but keep the answer ready.
- Say what happens if the reader says no, in the user's own figures. The reader weighs the request against doing nothing, and a piece that leaves that side blank lets them assume doing nothing costs nothing.
- Do not label the parts in the finished piece. The reader should see an argument, not a diagram.

### Reading the draft as the reader

Before the user sees the draft, read it once as the reader you described when planning, with their grievance, their alternative and their patience. Do not skip this for a short piece. Look for each of these and fix what you find.

1. **What they came for.** The thing this reader wants to know or fears hearing (what happens to me, what will it cost, has the thing I complained about changed) is answered in the opening lines, plainly, before the case for anything.
2. **Lines that make this reader bristle.** Telling them how they will feel. Calling a cost to them small. A hint of guilt or threat. A demand they have already refused. Praise of the writer that the reader would dispute. Reword or cut. With a reader who believes they have behaved well, ask for something they can say yes to.
3. **What they know that the page hides.** The writer's stake in the outcome, a past failure, the awkward number. If the reader knows it or will find it, the piece says it first.
4. **Answers they can hold the writer to.** Where the piece answers a worry, the answer names something the reader can check: a date, a number, a person, what happens if it is missed. One such commitment does more than three assurances.
5. **Every number, date, name and promise traces to the user.** Go through them one at a time. Each is something the user gave, or arithmetic on it that you have redone. Anything else comes out, or stays as a proposal worded as one. Leave no placeholder in the body where a sentence built from the given facts would do.
6. **They can find it.** A reader who will scan for terms (pay, dates, price, what to do next) finds them set apart where the eye lands. A piece that will be spoken has short sentences and one number to a sentence.
7. **Length and form** are what the user asked for.

Hand over the revised piece. Outside it, list anything you proposed that the user has to confirm.

### Checking an argument

For the user's own draft or someone else's piece. First write out the claim, grounds and warrant as the piece actually has them. If the warrant is unstated, write the most plausible one the author could be relying on, and judge the argument by its strongest reading.

Then ask, in this order:

1. **Claim.** Can it be read more than one way? From what standpoint is it made (financial, moral, legal, practical), and is that the standpoint this reader cares about?
2. **Rebuttal.** Does the wording of the claim allow for the exceptions? If a known exception would make the claim false as written, the claim is overstated.
3. **Backing.** Is the warrant sound in general, and does it apply to this case?
4. **Grounds.** Are they enough, are they relevant, and will this reader accept them as fact without further support?

The fastest single test: **write the unstated warrant as one plain sentence and ask whether the reader would agree with it.** Most arguments that feel persuasive and are not sound fail at this point.

Also check whether the same grounds would support a rival claim just as well. If they would, the argument has not yet shown why this claim and not that one.

Five quick questions catch most faults: Are grounds really being offered, or does the support only restate the claim? Do they bear directly on the claim? Is there enough evidence? Does the argument assume something the reader does not share? Is any key word used in two senses? `references/toulmin-book.md` lists the named faults under each question.

Look at what kind of link the argument relies on, and apply the tests for that kind from the same file. A comparison fails on one relevant difference. A general claim needs a sample that is large and typical. A causal claim needs a mechanism and has to rule out a third factor. An appeal to an expert needs the expert named, qualified in this field, and weighed against other experts.

A fault is seldom fatal. The repair is to make the claim narrower or more exact, find grounds that bear on it more directly, and then restate the warrant to fit.

When the argument is about right and wrong, look hardest at how the facts are described. The principle is rarely in dispute. Whether an act counts as "lying" or as "loyalty" is what settles it.

When judging a claim that turned out wrong, keep two faults apart. A claim can have been properly made on what was known then and still be mistaken. A claim that was made with no standing or no grounds was improper even if it came out right.

Report what you find in terms of the piece itself, not in the model's vocabulary, unless the user is working with the model directly. Say "the piece never says why a drop in turnover would raise profit, and a reader outside the industry will not fill that in," not "the warrant is unstated."

## When the evidence supports less than the claim

The user wants to say something bold and the grounds carry only part of it.

Before you decide there is a gap, work out everything the user's facts can carry. Derive every figure the facts allow: rates, totals, the pace a target needs set against the pace already reached, and what happens if nothing changes. A trend the user gave you is a ground. If a figure the user gave has been moving in one direction, compare the proposal with that figure carried forward on its trend, not held at last year's level. Then put each true ground in the form most favourable to the claim. For a climbing gym that needs to sell 120 annual passes this year, "five times what the one-month sale reached" and "10 passes a month, when the sale sold 24 in one month" can be the same fact. Measure the gap only after this work, because some of it will have closed.

Do not narrow the claim without asking, and do not write the bold version without saying anything. Tell the user three things, briefly:

1. What the claim says and what the grounds actually support, in one sentence each.
2. A narrower or qualified version of the claim that the grounds do support. The qualification has to name a real condition: something that could turn out either way and that the reader can check. A condition that only restates the claim in other units is empty. "We cover the $54,000 rent rise if we sell 120 annual passes at $450" says nothing, because 120 passes is $54,000. Where the reader has to decide, give the condition a checkpoint: a date, a number to reach by then, and what the writer will do if it is missed.
3. What a sceptical reader would say to the bold version. Test each objection against the user's own facts before you pass it on. If the facts already answer it, do not offer it as a reason to retreat. Put it in the piece as an objection with its answer. Pass on only the objections the facts cannot answer.

Then write whichever version the user picks. The decision is theirs. A fundraising ask and a board memo call for different amounts of caution, and the user knows the reader.

Thin grounds have two fixes: qualify the claim, or find stronger grounds. A claim stated flat on one example fails. The same example is fine in support of "this can work."

## How much to qualify and how many exceptions to state

There is a cost on each side. With too few, the reader finds the holes. With too many, the argument drowns in small print. Three things decide it: who the audience is, what the setting is for, and what the piece is meant to do. A board that has to decide today needs a recommendation it can act on, with the one or two exceptions that would change the decision. A research audience expects a tentative claim and every caveat.

Match the qualifier to the backing. Strong backing lets the claim stand without a qualifier. Weak backing supports only "may" or "it is possible that."

"Probably" is a commitment with a guard on it, and not a way to avoid one. Use it only when you can name what you are guarding against, and expect to be held to the claim if that thing does not happen. A flat statement puts the writer's word behind the claim. A claim hedged with nothing behind the hedge reads as evasion.

Do not apologise for an argument because it is not a proof. Outside mathematics no argument is. The standard is the one that fits the field: a case strong enough that doubting it would be unreasonable.

Treat a rival view consistently. If it deserves a hearing, give it one. If it does not, say on what ground it is ruled out. Calling it possible and then ignoring it is the inconsistent move.

## What the model does not do

It does not supply facts. Finding good grounds and seeing the real exceptions takes knowledge of the subject. If the grounds are missing, say so and ask the user or go and research them. Do not invent them.

It does not make a weak case strong. It shows where the case is weak, which is the point of using it before the reader does.
